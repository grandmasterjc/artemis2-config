On Thursday evening, July 16, SpaceX counted down to the thirteenth test flight of Starship at Starbase in South Texas. At 6:45 p.m. EDT the 33 Raptor engines on the Super Heavy booster began to ignite — and then everything stopped. Onscreen telemetry showed four engines that never lit, the remaining engines shut down, and the world's largest rocket stayed bolted to the pad.

It was the first time a full-scale Starship has aborted at the moment of ignition. "We'll take some time, dig into what triggered that abort once the booster was igniting to launch, and then we'll figure out what our path forward is going to be," SpaceX's Dan Huot said on the launch webcast.

The path forward came a few hours later. Elon Musk wrote that evening that two Raptors will be removed and replaced, and that the most probable launch timing is early next week.

For anyone following Artemis, this is more than a scrubbed Starlink launch. The vehicle that stopped itself on Thursday is the same vehicle family NASA is counting on to land astronauts on the Moon.

## Twenty satellites and a heat-shield inspection

Flight 13 is the second flight of Starship Version 3, the upgraded variant SpaceX is using to push the megarocket toward operational status. The plan mirrors Flight 12 from May 22: send Super Heavy back for a controlled splashdown in the Gulf, and fly Ship most of the way around the world to a targeted splashdown off Western Australia.

The headline payload is new: 20 of SpaceX's next-generation Starlink V3 satellites, making their first trip to space. They are not meant to stay there — deployed on Ship's suborbital arc, they reenter after roughly 20 minutes. Six of them carry cameras pointed back at Ship's heat shield, turning the deployment into an inspection pass of the part of the vehicle SpaceX has struggled with longest.

Flight 12 counts as V3's mostly successful debut, with two caveats that Flight 13 is meant to retire: the booster did not manage its controlled Gulf splashdown, and Ship failed to relight one of its Raptors in space. Both are capabilities the program needs routinely, not occasionally.

## An abort that worked

There is a reasonable reading of Thursday in which the system did exactly what it should. Engine-out at ignition is a scenario every launch vehicle has to handle; the automation caught it in a fraction of a second, kept 33 engines' worth of methane and oxygen from going anywhere, and preserved the vehicle and the pad. Two engines get swapped, and the rocket flies days later. That is what a maturing program looks like.

The comparison a few hundred kilometers east makes the point. When Blue Origin's New Glenn failed on the pad in May, it took the vehicle, the transporter-erector and much of Launch Complex 36A with it, and the rebuild now stretches toward the end of the year. Starship's version of a bad day on the pad, this week at least, costs two engines and a few days.

## The lander is waiting on a launch rate

The catch is that the Artemis schedule does not need Starship to be safe on the pad. It needs Starship to be fast.

Starship HLS — the lander variant of Ship — is what NASA astronauts will ride from lunar orbit down to the surface on Artemis IV, the mission now carrying the landing after NASA moved Artemis III to a low-Earth-orbit demonstration flight. Before any of that, SpaceX has two program-defining tests targeted for this year on V3 hardware: a long-duration flight and the first in-space transfer of cryogenic propellant between vehicles. Propellant transfer is the make-or-break technology — the lander only reaches the Moon by tanking up in Earth orbit from multiple Starship launches.

That architecture is a cadence bet. Refueling a single lunar lander takes a series of launches in quick succession, which means the program's real currency is launches per month, not launches per year. Every week that slips between test flights pushes the propellant-transfer demonstration to the right, and the demonstration is what stands between the current schedule and the 2028 landing.

One pad abort does not move 2028. But V3 has now flown once in May and stood down once in July, and the year is half over. The margin the program was supposed to build up this summer is being spent on the ground.

## Two Raptors now, two bigger tests later

The near-term steps are concrete. Watch for the two replaced Raptors and a new launch attempt early next week; then whether Super Heavy finally sticks its Gulf splashdown and Ship repeats its Australia arrival; then, later this year, the long-duration flight and the propellant-transfer demonstration that Artemis IV actually depends on. If those two happen in 2026, Thursday's abort will be a footnote. If they slip into 2027, it will look like part of a pattern.
