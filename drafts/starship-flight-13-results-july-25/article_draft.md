At 22:51 UTC on Friday, on the third attempt in nine days, Starship Flight 13 left the pad at Starbase. Sixty-seven minutes later the upper stage was lying on its side in the Indian Ocean, west of Australia. That part always happens. What has never happened before is what came next: nothing. No fireball, no breakup. The vehicle just floated there, belly up in the swell, still sending telemetry.

"That is a first," SpaceX's Dan Huot said on the webcast, as a drone circled the bobbing stainless-steel hull. "That is the softest splashdown we have ever had with Starship in the Indian Ocean."

By the standards of a program that measures progress in what survives, Flight 13 was the best Starship flight yet. It needed to be. This is the vehicle NASA is counting on to land the Artemis IV crew on the Moon in 2028, and until Friday its 2026 record consisted of one crash, one pad abort and a weather scrub. So yes, a very good night. It still leaves the lander program short of the thing Artemis actually requires, and the gap is bigger than the celebration suggests. Here is the scorecard, including the part that should worry NASA.

## Twenty satellites through the slot

The headline payload worked without drama, which is itself the story. One after another, 20 Starlink V3 satellites slid out of the narrow payload bay door on Ship's suborbital arc. SpaceX has taken to calling the mechanism a Pez dispenser, and the description fits. The production version of this vehicle is meant to push out 60 at a time.

The last two satellites turned on small lights and looked back at the vehicle that had just released them, and six of the twenty spent their brief lives scanning Ship's heat shield from the outside. Those satellites reentered as planned about twenty minutes later. Their imagery did not.

The other quiet win happened in space: Ship relit one of its Raptors during the coast. SpaceX has struggled with that maneuver before, and it is not optional. A relight is how a future Starship leaves orbit on purpose, which is why nobody gets to orbit without proving it first.

## Five engines where thirteen should have been

Super Heavy's flight looked textbook through separation and the flip. Then came the landing burn, which called for 13 engines and got five. The booster hit the Gulf with, in Huot's careful phrasing, "quite a bit of velocity." The camera feed cut out on impact.

![Super Heavy during its landing burn on Flight 13](https://grandmasterjc.github.io/artemis2-config/updates/images/starship-flight-13-booster-burn.jpg)

It is the second booster landing miss in two V3 flights. On Flight 12 in May the booster crashed outright, so slowing down most of the way counts as movement in the right direction. But V3 boosters keep having engine trouble in exactly the phase where the eventual tower catch will demand perfection. Remember that this whole campaign started on July 16 with four Raptors refusing to light on the pad. Engine reliability, more than anything else, is the thread to pull on this vehicle.

## The Ship that lived to float

The reason SpaceX flew this mission on a deliberately punishing ascent profile, with load sensors bolted under the heat-shield tiles, is that the company still cannot fully explain how its shield behaves. A Ship that survives splashdown intact, engines and flaps visibly whole on the drone feed, is something the program has never had: hardware that can actually be examined instead of reconstructed from telemetry.

"This is a dream scenario for this team that's trying to get this heat shield data," Huot said. There were flames licking the nose for a few minutes after splashdown. They went out on their own. Whether SpaceX can recover the vehicle, or parts of it, from the middle of the Indian Ocean is another matter, but for the first time the option exists.

Elon Musk kept his summary short: intact, floating, transmitting.

## The line still missing from the checklist

Here is the uncomfortable number sitting under all the celebration: 13 flights, three years, zero completed orbits. Every Starship so far, this one included, has flown a suborbital arc.

That number is why NASA watches these flights the way it does. Starship is the Artemis IV lander, currently manifested to put astronauts on the Moon in 2028. Between here and there sit a full orbital flight, catching a returning Ship in the launch tower's arms, the in-space propellant transfer demonstration still penciled in for later this year, an orbital tanker, and a crewed lander with life support. NASA administrator Jared Isaacman congratulated the team on Friday and called Starship's future capabilities game-changing. He also knows that list better than anyone.

Friday shortened it, meaningfully. A working V3, a proven relight and a survivable reentry are prerequisites for everything else. And in a coincidence nobody at SpaceX planned, the flight flew 57 years to the day after the Apollo 11 crew came home from the Moon. The vehicle meant to take the next crews there finally had its best day.
