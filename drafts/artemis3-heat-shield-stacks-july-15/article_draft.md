On Saturday, July 11, teams at NASA's Kennedy Space Center secured the left- and right-hand aft assembly segments of the twin solid rocket boosters for Artemis III onto the mobile launcher inside the Vehicle Assembly Building. Less than three months after the Artemis II crew splashed down, the next stack has started to rise.

It is not happening in isolation. Inside the same building, SLS core stage processing is underway — teams joined the four-fifths rocket stage to its engine section in May, and the first RS-25 engines have arrived for installation. Kennedy also began running monthly launch countdown simulations on June 23, rehearsing the choreography of a launch day that is still more than a year out.

And last week, in the Neil A. Armstrong Operations and Checkout Building, technicians finished attaching the heat shield to the Artemis III Orion crew module. That is the piece worth stopping on.

Because the shield now bolted to Artemis III is not the same design that flew before. It was redesigned specifically to fix the problem that turned into the biggest scare of the entire Artemis program so far.

## The stack coming together at Kennedy

The booster stacking is the visible milestone, but the more telling detail is the mobile launcher underneath it. It is the same structure that launched Artemis II, now repaired and turned around for the next flight rather than replaced. The remaining booster segments will be transported to the VAB and stacked on top over the coming weeks.

The rest of the vehicle is converging on the same timeline. Once all four RS-25 engines arrive and are processed, teams will install them to complete the core stage, then integrate it atop the mobile launcher for testing. The countdown simulations that began in June are the ground team's version of the same idea: run the sequence enough times that launch day is routine.

For a program that spends a lot of its life in reformulation slides, this is refreshingly physical. Three months after Artemis II, there is a real rocket going vertical.

## The heat shield that scared everyone

To understand why the heat shield matters, go back to Artemis I. In December 2022, an uncrewed Orion returned from the Moon and hit the atmosphere at about 40,000 kilometers per hour in the first US use of a skip entry — a maneuver that dips into the atmosphere, skips back out to shed heat, then reenters. When the capsule was recovered, inspectors found something they did not expect: chunks of the Avcoat ablative material had come off the heat shield.

NASA spent two years running it down. The investigation, concluded in December 2024, found that gases generated inside the Avcoat as it heated could not escape fast enough. Trapped, they expanded and blew pieces off the shield — a process called spalling — made worse by the cool-down between the two heating phases of the skip entry. The root cause was permeability: the material was not venting evenly.

![Artemis I heat shield showing char loss after recovery](https://grandmasterjc.github.io/artemis2-config/updates/images/artemis1-heat-shield-damage.jpg)

For Artemis II, NASA chose not to rebuild the shield. It kept the existing hardware and instead changed the reentry trajectory to keep Orion out of the conditions that caused the trapped-gas buildup. That worked — the crew came home safely in April — but it was a mitigation, a way to fly around the flaw rather than remove it.

## What is different on Artemis III

Artemis III is where the fix itself becomes flight hardware. Orion's new heat shield is built from 186 blocks of Avcoat, each individually inspected, and the design was upgraded to achieve uniformity and consistent permeability across those blocks — so the gases vent evenly instead of building up and spalling. NASA credits the change directly to the analysis it did on the Artemis I shield.

There is an honest caveat worth stating. Artemis III, under NASA's revised sequence, is the crewed demonstration flight in low Earth orbit — the docking rehearsal we covered when the crew was named, with the first lunar landing pushed to Artemis IV. A reentry from low Earth orbit is far gentler than the 40,000-kilometer-per-hour return that damaged the Artemis I shield. So Artemis III will carry the corrected design, but it will not fully stress-test it. The definitive proof at lunar-return speed waits for the missions after it.

Still, this is the first Orion built with the fixed shield rather than a worked-around one. Every crewed lunar return that follows depends on it behaving the way the redesign intends.

## Where this leaves us

The next milestones are concrete and close together: the remaining booster segments stacking in the VAB, the RS-25 engines going in to finish the core stage, and the crew and service modules being integrated with the shielded Orion — all pointing toward a 2027 launch. Watch the heat-shield language especially. "Installed" and "proven" are different words, and the gap between them is where the Artemis I lesson still lives.
