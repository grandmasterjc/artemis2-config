The day before NASA's Oct. 23 briefing at Kennedy Space Center in Florida, technicians lifted the Artemis 3 core stage upright inside the Vehicle Assembly Building, then laid it on its side again to weigh it. The stage is about 220 feet (67 meters) long and 28 feet (8.5 meters) wide, and it holds more than 733,000 gallons (2.8 million liters) of liquid hydrogen and liquid oxygen. Engineers need its exact weight to finish the flight design.

The mission that stage will launch no longer goes to the moon. NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman, Associate Administrator Amit Kshatriya and Artemis program manager Jeremy Parsons spent about two hours explaining the new Artemis 3 and the changes to Artemis 4, the flight now meant to land astronauts in 2028. This article follows their presentation and the questions that came after it, and marks which parts are demonstrated hardware and which are still plans.

## Core stage on the scale

According to Parsons, the twin solid rocket boosters were stacked at the end of September, and the core stage is due to be joined to them in the coming weeks. The assembled booster-and-core "short stack," without Orion or an upper stage, will then roll to Launch Pad 39B for a propellant-loading test that Isaacman put at year-end. Artemis 3 itself is targeted for summer 2027.

Parsons credited embedded NASA staff for processing times cut in half in some cases and hardware non-conformances resolved up to 85 percent faster. The mobile launcher was back in the Vehicle Assembly Building 17 days after Artemis 2 lifted off.

The test targets a part that leaked on Artemis 1 and 2, the liquid hydrogen quick disconnect. Fixing it through the usual contracts was expected to cost tens of millions of dollars and take years. NASA redesigned and built a replacement in-house in under five months, Parsons said, and it is already on the vehicle. A second set is in hydrogen flow testing at Stennis Space Center in Mississippi, where low-flow cryogenic tests showed almost no leakage.

Because Artemis 3 stays in low Earth orbit, a spacer built at Marshall Space Flight Center in Alabama replaces the upper stage. Orion is more than 95 percent integrated, and two Starlink terminals were being installed this week to improve the imagery Orion sends home.

## Fourteen days in orbit

The mission is baselined at 14 days and can stretch to 17 if weather delays the launch. Blue Origin goes first: a New Glenn rocket places a Blue Moon test lander in a roughly 237-nautical-mile (273-mile, 439-kilometer) orbit, where it can wait up to a month. Orion follows on SLS, and after two days of checkout it meets the lander on flight day 3.

The crew spends two days docked, and unlike every later step they open the hatch and go inside. They check the lander's air and life support, test emergency scenarios and practice flying two joined vehicles. On flight day 6 a Starship test vehicle launches from Florida. Orion undocks, drops to a 206-nautical-mile orbit and meets Starship on flight day 8, where a docking system built into Starship's nose cone allows a test of about two hours. Nobody enters Starship. NASA wants to measure how the two craft handle docking loads and attitude control, since Starship is far larger than Orion.

The remaining days are filled hour by hour, Parsons said. They include survival suit evaluations that Artemis 2 did not complete, docking sensor tests and acoustic and thermal measurements. Randy Bresnik, Frank Rubio, Andre Douglas and ESA's Luca Parmitano, assigned June 9, have logged more than 350 hours of classroom training and 22 integrated simulations with flight controllers.

Both landers are test articles. Blue Origin's crew cabin is only now being welded, with pressure testing and full integration of its Mark 2 test lander slated for early 2027.

## The refueling count

A reporter raised an estimate of roughly 22 Starship refueling flights per lunar landing, a figure SpaceX chief executive Elon Musk had put at 6 to 8 on X. Parsons said NASA is not tracking anywhere near 22, and that 6 to 8 is closer to what SpaceX is planning. The final number depends on how much each Starship carries and how much propellant is lost in transfer, which NASA conservatively puts at 5 to 10 percent.

None of that is measured yet. SpaceX is targeting early 2027 for its first propellant transfer between two Starships, launched from Starbase in Texas and from Kennedy. NASA also said the next Starship flight, expected in November, may bring the ship and booster back to the launch site to be caught.

## Two routes to the surface

For Artemis 4, the plan has changed on both sides. Under the Starship route, the lander is fueled in Earth orbit before Orion launches, then docks with Orion in high Earth orbit and fires the translunar injection burn itself, pushing both vehicles toward low lunar orbit. NASA has approved the Orion modifications for that push and says fabrication is underway. SpaceX is targeting an uncrewed lunar landing demonstration at the end of 2027 and a first crewed landing in the second half of 2028.

![The two Artemis 4 landing routes. Source: NASA, Oct. 23, 2026.](https://grandmasterjc.github.io/artemis2-config/updates/images/artemis-acceleration-briefing-october-25-routes.jpg)

Blue Origin's route sends three transfer vehicles derived from its Mark 1 lander, followed by the Mark 2, into an elliptical polar lunar orbit, where Orion's crew transfers to the lander. Blue's uncrewed Mark 1 cargo lander is due to fly next year, and the company says pad operations will restart in November, with New Glenn returning to flight soon after. Parsons said NASA has dropped the option of launching Blue's Artemis 3 lander on another rocket. NASA intends to delay choosing which company lands the first crew for as long as the data allows.

## Suit and upper stage

Two hardware changes serve both routes. The "sortie" version of Axiom Space's lunar suit is meant to cut mass from a little over 500 pounds (227 kilograms) to about 250 pounds (113 kilograms), with a minimum of two hours per moonwalk. Parsons said the first landing plans two such moonwalks, and the pressure garment is due this year.

![Sortie suit and stretched Centaur compared with their baselines. Source: NASA, Oct. 23, 2026.](https://grandmasterjc.github.io/artemis2-config/updates/images/artemis-acceleration-briefing-october-25-suit-centaur.jpg)

The second is a stretched Centaur upper stage for later SLS flights, carrying about 50 percent more propellant than the standard Centaur V and delivering 41 to 43 metric tons to the moon, according to Parsons. NASA will choose between it and the existing Interim Cryogenic Propulsion Stage around next summer.

## Milestones through 2027

Parsons said Artemis 3 is about 90 days behind. Supply chain items drive the slip, and avionics for the Blue Origin test lander are the longest delay. To recover 23 days on one lander dome, NASA moved it across the country on a military C-17 cargo plane. Isaacman said the 90 days can shrink and that the figure should be read as encouraging, not alarming.

From here the calendar runs roughly in this order: Blue Origin's pad restart and Starship's next flight in November, the short-stack tanking test by year-end, Mark 2 test lander integration and the first Starship propellant transfer in early 2027, an Artemis 3 launch in summer 2027, and Starship's uncrewed lunar demonstration at the end of that year.

![Milestones from November 2026 to 2028. Source: NASA, Oct. 23, 2026.](https://grandmasterjc.github.io/artemis2-config/updates/images/artemis-acceleration-briefing-october-25-milestones.jpg)

Of everything on that calendar, the weighed core stage is the only item with a measured result so far.
