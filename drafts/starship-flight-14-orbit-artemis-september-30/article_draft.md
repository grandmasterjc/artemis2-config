At 8:48 a.m. Eastern time on Sept. 28, a Starship lifted off from Starbase, Texas. About 22 minutes later, flight controllers called it "go for orbit." For the first time in 14 flights, the ship did not fly an arc that dropped back into the ocean. It stayed up, roughly 170 miles (275 kilometers) above Earth, and circled the planet.

It did so with one of its three vacuum-optimized Raptor engines dead, [according to Astronomy magazine's account](https://www.astronomy.com/whats-launching-this-week/starship-reaches-orbit-on-14th-test-flight/). The flight ended early, after 3 hours, 8 minutes and 32 seconds, and the ship broke apart six seconds after splashdown in the Pacific. SpaceX still called it a success. What does a first orbit change for the National Aeronautics and Space Administration's (NASA) plans to return to the moon?

## The flight itself

Flight 14 carried the first operational payload Starship has flown: 26 Starlink V3 satellites. All of them were released by about 10 a.m., Astronomy reported. SpaceX communications manager Dan Huot said the deployment equaled "20 Falcon 9 flights in terms of bandwidth." Elon Musk posted that the "first orbital flight of Starship" was successful, according to 24/7 Wall St.

The ship lost a vacuum Raptor shortly after separating from the Super Heavy booster. Controllers paused the orbital insertion, checked the data, and concluded the remaining engines could still finish the job, including the burn needed to come back down. The booster lost one of its 33 engines about a minute after liftoff and splashed down in the Gulf of Mexico. Because of the engine loss, SpaceX ended the mission early. The plan had been for about six orbits.

Huot acknowledged the ending was rougher than the previous flight's. "Most importantly, Starship made it to orbit," he said.

## Orbital speed

All 13 earlier flights were suborbital. A ship on that path is moving fast, but not fast enough to stay up. Orbit requires about 17,500 miles per hour (28,000 kilometers per hour), and whatever reaches it has to survive the trip down again.

The distinction matters for Artemis because NASA's next crewed mission depends on it. Artemis 3 will stay in Earth orbit, not go to the moon. According to [NASA's mission outline](https://www.nasa.gov/missions/artemis/artemis-3/nasa-outlines-preliminary-artemis-iii-mission-plans/), the four-person Orion crew will rendezvous and dock with test versions of the commercial landers from SpaceX and Blue Origin. A Starship that could not reach orbit could not be part of that rendezvous at all.

## The Artemis 3 test

The mission is a rehearsal, and it has slipped: Space.com reported it had been pushed to late 2027. NASA has been clear about its purpose. Jeremy Parsons, acting assistant deputy administrator for Moon to Mars at NASA, said it would "help us learn how Orion, the crew, and ground teams all interact together" before the astronauts go to the surface on Artemis 4, which targets the south pole region. The agency has said astronauts will not enter the Starship test vehicle, but will dock with it.

Flight 14 therefore answers a narrow question. Can a Starship get into a stable orbit and come back under control? On Sept. 28, the answer was yes, with one engine out. The docking-equipped Starship for that flight still needs to be built, launched and proven, and Flight 14's payload was satellites, not a docking port.

## Refueling, still unproven

The harder step comes later. A Starship that lands astronauts on the moon has to be filled with propellant in Earth orbit first, because one launch cannot lift a fully fueled ship all the way to the moon. That means a second ship transferring super-cold liquid oxygen and methane to the first, in weightlessness. Astronomy noted this transfer of cryogenic propellant between spacecraft has not been demonstrated.

NASA also requires SpaceX to fly an uncrewed demonstration before a crew rides the lander, the agency says on its [human landing systems page](https://www.nasa.gov/reference/human-landing-systems/). That demonstration comes after orbit, after refueling and after a landing on the moon. Flight 14 is the first of those steps, and the only one so far that has flown.

## Flights ahead

The next Starship flight, number 15, is provisionally listed no earlier than Oct. 19, according to its Wikipedia entry, which also says SpaceX had not confirmed a date as of Sept. 28. That flight is expected to begin testing the docking and hatch mechanism for propellant transfer and to attempt the first catch of a returning ship by the launch tower. If those go well, a full ship-to-ship transfer with two vehicles in orbit follows, and then the uncrewed landing demonstration NASA requires.

NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman said in August, [according to Spaceflight Now](https://spaceflightnow.com/2026/08/14/nasa-administrator-extremely-confident-in-artemis-3-launch-in-2027/), that launches every couple of weeks through the end of the year would make the agency "feel very good" about SpaceX's contribution to Artemis 3. One orbital flight does not make a cadence, and SpaceX has not yet said what caused the engine loss.

Reuse is the other open item. Starship has not been caught, reflown or flown repeatedly, 24/7 Wall St. noted, and SpaceX has said it plans to try catching both stages before the end of 2026. Artemis needs many Starship launches for each landing, so launch cost and turnaround time matter as much as the orbit itself. NASA currently targets 2028 for the first crewed landing.
