On August 31, NASA's Aerospace Safety Advisory Panel (ASAP) held its third quarterly public meeting of 2026, and panel member Paul Hill delivered the numbers Orion's heat shield had been building toward since Artemis I splashed down in 2022. Artemis I's shield came home with more than 100 sites of cracked, missing char material, known as spallation. Artemis II's had nine. Hill told the panel that was a reduction of more than 90 percent, and NASA achieved it without touching the heat shield itself.

That detail is the one worth sitting with. NASA did not fly a redesigned shield on Artemis II in April. It flew the same Avcoat-coated shield already bolted to the capsule and changed only the path Orion took through the atmosphere on the way down. The choice split the people who study heat shields for a living. Some said flying without a hardware fix was too much risk to carry a crew through, months before anyone had the reentry data to settle it.

Now Kennedy Space Center has that data, and it has moved on to the next capsule. Solid rocket booster segments for Artemis III are more than halfway stacked in the Vehicle Assembly Building, alongside a heat shield built with the material fix Artemis II flew without.

## The permeability problem

Artemis I flew uncrewed in 2022 using a skip entry, in which Orion dips into the atmosphere, rises back out on lift, then commits to final descent. NASA's investigation, published in December 2024 by Orion program manager Howard Hu and Moon to Mars deputy associate administrator Amit Kshatriya, traced the damage to that pause in the middle of the profile. As Orion climbed back out of the atmosphere, heating rates dropped and thermal energy already built up inside the Avcoat kept generating gas. Avcoat is designed to char and vent that gas as it burns away. Where the material was not porous enough, the gas had nowhere to go, pressure built beneath the surface, and chunks of char cracked off. NASA's investigators found a useful control group by accident: patches of Avcoat that happened to be more permeable than the rest of the shield came through Artemis I's reentry without cracking.

That finding pointed to two possible fixes: build a new, uniformly permeable heat shield, or fly a different reentry path that never lets heat soak in long enough to cause the problem. Swapping the shield would have meant pulling one already installed on the Artemis II capsule and delaying a mission that had already slipped past its original target. NASA chose the trajectory instead, flying a steeper, non-skip reentry that traded a longer, gentler ride for one that got Orion through the atmosphere before trapped gas could build much pressure.

## The case against flying

Public pushback followed, from people with direct experience of what happens when NASA is wrong about a heat shield. Charlie Camarda, a former astronaut who flew Space Shuttle Discovery on the 2005 return-to-flight mission after Columbia broke apart on reentry, said in January that flying crew on a vehicle with a known defective heat shield was irresponsible. Ed Pope, a materials engineer who has examined the Artemis I damage, put the odds of a serious failure on Artemis II somewhere between one in five and one in fifty, and argued NASA's trajectory fix addressed the wrong variable. Space historian Jordan Bimm noted separately that Avcoat had flown on Orion since 2009 with limited real-world reentry testing beyond Artemis I itself.

NASA's leadership did not dispute that Artemis I's damage was real. Engineers had reproduced the char-loss pattern in arc jet facilities at NASA's Ames Research Center, and Administrator Jared Isaacman said that work left him with full confidence in the spacecraft and its heat shield. Danny Olivas, a former astronaut who sat on the independent review of the Artemis I findings, had initially called the unmodified shield unsuitable for a crew; after reviewing the full analysis, he came around to calling the flight plan's risk acceptable.

## The post-flight numbers

Orion splashed down in the Pacific off San Diego on April 10, four astronauts aboard: commander Reid Wiseman, pilot Victor Glover, mission specialist Christina Koch and Canadian Space Agency astronaut Jeremy Hansen. Reentry topped out at nearly 25,000 mph (40,000 km/h), and Orion crossed the entry interface within 1 mph (1.6 km/h) of the predicted velocity, splashing down 2.9 miles (4.7 kilometers) from its target point in the ocean. Navy divers photographed the heat shield underwater within hours, and the char pattern they found matched what arc-jet testing on the ground had predicted.

Hill's spallation count, presented five months later, is the fullest account yet of what that pattern amounted to: nine sites, versus more than 100 on Artemis I, using the same shield design and the same Avcoat batch, with the only variable being the descent path. It does not resolve every question critics raised before the flight. Pope's objection was partly about the shield's underlying structure, not just its trajectory exposure, and nothing about a clean reentry rules out a bad outcome on a future flight with different conditions. But the specific bet NASA made, that a trajectory change alone could cut the damage dramatically without a new heat shield, is no longer a projection. It is a measured result.

## The next real test

The heat shield already attached to Artemis III's Orion crew module is not the one that flew on Artemis II. It was built at NASA's Michoud Assembly Facility to the more uniform permeability standard the investigation identified, the fix NASA skipped for Artemis II because there was no time to install it. That makes Artemis III the first mission to fly the corrected material. It will not, however, be the mission that proves it works under the conditions that caused the original problem. NASA restructured Artemis III in February to send Orion no farther than a parking orbit roughly 286 miles (460 kilometers) above Earth, where it will dock with test versions of SpaceX's Starship and Blue Origin's Blue Moon landers rather than fly to the moon and back. A return from that altitude does not approach lunar-return reentry speeds, and it will not use the skip profile that overheated Artemis I's shield in the first place.

Its real trial comes with Artemis IV, the mission that now carries the lunar landing NASA moved off Artemis III, still targeted for early 2028. That flight is when a corrected heat shield meets the same skip trajectory and the same lunar-return heating that damaged Artemis I and forced NASA to fly around the problem on Artemis II rather than through it.

The argument over Artemis II was never really about whether the spacecraft would survive one reentry. It was about whether NASA understood its own hardware well enough to bet a crew's safety on a trajectory change instead of a rebuild. Nine spallation sites against more than 100 is a specific, measured answer to that question, not a final one. The fix behind it still has to fly the entry profile it was actually designed for, and that is still more than a year away.
