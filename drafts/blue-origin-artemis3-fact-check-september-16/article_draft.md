On Aug. 14, Jared Isaacman, administrator of the National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA), walked the assembly line at Blue Origin's Florida factory, inspecting hardware bound for the company's Artemis 3 test lander. He told reporters at NASA's Max Power Expo that he was "extremely confident" the mission would launch in 2027, according to [Spaceflight Now](https://spaceflightnow.com/2026/08/14/nasa-administrator-extremely-confident-in-artemis-3-launch-in-2027/). It was a small, deliberate piece of stagecraft: the head of the agency touring a supplier's floor to project confidence in a program that, three months earlier, multiple outlets had all but written off.

That earlier verdict came fast. Within a day of Blue Origin's New Glenn rocket exploding on a Cape Canaveral pad, Wendy Whitman Cobb, a professor of strategy and security studies at Air University, wrote in [The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/blue-origin-rocket-exploded-on-launchpad-throwing-the-future-of-nasas-artemis-program-into-question-284133) that "Blue Origin's inability to launch Blue Moon anytime soon is likely to put the company out of the running for Artemis III," adding that NASA's "entire lunar exploration program is likely to be dependent on SpaceX for the time being." Fortune and other outlets repeated the framing, and it has stuck around since, still surfacing whenever the mission comes up. NASA's own record over the following four months does not support it, though the schedule risk behind it hasn't gone away either. Both things can be true, and untangling them means going back to what NASA actually said, in order.

## The May explosion

The rocket that exploded on May 28 wasn't carrying any Artemis hardware. New Glenn vehicle NG-4 was going through a static fire test at Launch Complex 36 ahead of a planned June 4 launch of Amazon Leo satellites, Jeff Bezos's other space venture, according to [Spaceflight Now](https://spaceflightnow.com/2026/05/29/blue-origins-new-glenn-rocket-explodes-during-prelaunch-testing-at-cape-canaveral/). The satellites hadn't even been moved to the pad yet. Blue Origin CEO Dave Limp later traced the cause to the main oxygen valve on one of the rocket's BE-4 engines, confirmed by hardware recovery and inspection, [Space.com reported](https://www.space.com/space-exploration/launches-spacecraft/blue-origin-traces-origin-of-huge-new-glenn-rocket-explosion-to-oxygen-valve-ceo-says) in early August.

What the explosion did damage was the only launch pad New Glenn has. That single fact, not any lunar hardware lost in the blast, is the entire basis for every Artemis 3 concern that followed. Blue Moon Mark 1, a robotic cargo lander, and Blue Moon Mark 2, the crewed lander due to rendezvous with Orion for Artemis 3, both fly on New Glenn from that same pad. Wreck the pad, and both landers' launch dates move, whether or not either one was ever near the fire.

## A number without its context

Isaacman gave the "out of the running" narrative its most durable piece of ammunition less than a week later. In a June 1 interview, he told CNBC that a 2028 timeframe was "within the realm" of possible pad recovery, saying, "we certainly want to see Blue Origin be very successful. So recovering, getting the pad recovered, providing subject matter expertise, root cause analysis for sure," per [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/06/01/blue-origin-launchpad-may-not-be-restored-until-2028-nasas-isaacman.html). Outlets from CGTN to Seeking Alpha ran versions of the same headline: the pad might not be fixed until 2028, a full year past Artemis 3's target window.

A dispute over that reading followed soon after. In a post on his official X account, Isaacman wrote that the question he'd been answering was actually about the timing of NASA's Moon Base program and its recent lunar rover contract awards, not the pad: "I was pointing out that those missions are not until 2028, which should be well within what is possible for pad recovery." Whether the original answer supports that gloss is a matter of interpretation. What isn't in dispute is that a single ambiguous sentence, stripped of the question that prompted it, is what most of the "Blue Origin is done" coverage that summer actually rested on.

## Decoupling the lander

NASA hedged anyway. Two days after the CNBC interview, Isaacman told Fox Business, "We are also de-coupling the lander from the launch vehicle and the pad itself," and a NASA spokesperson said the agency wanted to move Blue Moon launches "to a rocket that's not New Glenn," according to [Spaceflight Now](https://spaceflightnow.com/2026/06/04/nasa-head-urges-new-launcher-for-blue-origins-moon-landers-to-meet-artemis-mission-deadlines/). The options are thin. Blue Moon was designed around New Glenn's 23-foot (7-meter) payload fairing; SpaceX's Falcon Heavy carries a narrower one, just 17 feet (5.2 meters), and its pads lack the hydrogen-fueling infrastructure Blue Moon's engines require. Isaacman acknowledged the constraint directly: "In terms of heavy lift, you know, real heavy lift, you've got, SpaceX and Blue Origin." No alternate rocket has been named since.

## Confidence from the top

NASA's public position, meanwhile, never wavered from support. On June 9, Jeremy Parsons, acting assistant deputy associate administrator of NASA's Moon to Mars Program Office, said flatly that "NASA remains confident that New Glenn will be ready for Artemis III," and Blue Origin's John Couluris, senior vice president of lunar permanence, said the company's Artemis 3 vehicle would be "completed and ready for launch in 2027," according to [RD World Online](https://www.rdworldonline.com/nasa-is-confident-blue-origin-will-be-ready-for-artemis-iii-despite-explosion/).

By June 30, Limp had settled on a rebuild plan: a hybrid horizontal-vertical integration system replacing the transporter-erector destroyed in the blast, with pad operations targeted to resume before the end of 2026, [Spaceflight Now reported](https://spaceflightnow.com/2026/06/30/blue-origin-outlines-return-to-flight-logistics-for-its-new-glenn-rockets/). That timeline already cost the Blue Moon Mark 1 cargo mission its 2026 launch date, pushing it to early 2027. Carlos García-Galán, NASA's Moon Base program executive, said the agency was "working with Blue Origin very closely to understand their timelines to recovery" and was still weighing whether to fly Mark 1 on an alternate rocket instead of waiting. Isaacman called Blue Origin's recovery progress "almost beyond impressive." His August factory visit was the same message delivered in person.

## Outside the agency

Not everyone finds the optimism convincing. Veteran SpaceX employees who worked the aftermath of the 2016 AMOS-6 pad explosion, a comparable static fire failure that also destroyed a launch complex, told Ars Technica that Limp's end-of-2026 target was unrealistic, with industry estimates for the repair running past a year. If that estimate is closer to right than Blue Origin's own, New Glenn may not fly again until well into 2027, on a pad still short of the reliability record a crewed program would want. NASA's search for a rocket that isn't New Glenn looks less like caution and more like necessity from that angle.

## The dates that matter

Two dates will settle more of this than any statement has. The first is whether Blue Origin actually returns New Glenn to flight before the end of 2026, as Limp has said it will; a slip past that would confirm the skeptics NASA has so far declined to engage directly. The second is whether that return happens with enough margin to fly the Blue Moon Mark 2 test lander ahead of Artemis 3's own launch window, expected as soon as mid-2027. NASA's decision on an alternate rocket, if one ever comes, would be the clearest signal that the agency's private assessment has shifted from its public one.

NASA never removed Blue Origin from the Artemis 3 plan, and nothing in the record shows the agency seriously considered it. The verdict that it had was published a day after the explosion, before Blue Origin had even identified what failed. Four months later, NASA is still working on a fallback launcher it hasn't had to use, and New Glenn still hasn't flown since the fire that grounded it. Isaacman's assembly-line tour showed confidence. Whether that confidence is warranted depends on a launch pad in Florida that, as of this writing, has not put a rocket back in the air.
