NASA sets a numerical limit on how likely it will accept losing a crew. For the Artemis missions as a whole the limit is 1 in 30. For the lunar operations alone, the landing, the stay and the launch back to orbit, it is 1 in 40. By comparison, NASA's Commercial Crew Program holds trips to the International Space Station to 1 in 500 for launch and return.

Those figures come from a March 2026 audit by NASA's Office of Inspector General (OIG), report IG-26-004. The same audit notes that the Space Shuttle was believed to fly at 1 in 100 and was later calculated at 1 in 10 for its early flights. Which parts of the program carry the most danger, and what has been done about each?

## The lander

The OIG's verdict is direct: of all the systems Artemis needs, the Human Landing System (HLS) "carries the highest probability of crew loss." A lander has to descend to the surface, keep two astronauts alive for days, and fly them back up to dock with Orion. Each of those steps is new.

NASA's mitigations are concrete. Both SpaceX and Blue Origin must fly an uncrewed demonstration that includes a landing and a lunar ascent before a crew boards. The OIG still found gaps. NASA and SpaceX disagree about whether the Starship design meets the agency's manual control requirement, which exists because Apollo crews used their backup manual control on every one of the program's seven planned landings. And the audit states plainly that NASA has no way to rescue a crew stranded in lunar orbit or on the surface. A second Orion on standby, or a spare lander on the moon, would be too expensive.

The OIG also reported that SpaceX's lander would not be ready for a June 2027 landing. NASA has since restructured the plan. Artemis 3 is now an Earth-orbit docking test, and the first landing is assigned to Artemis 4, which NASA targets for 2028.

## Refueling in orbit

Starship cannot carry enough propellant to leave Earth orbit full. According to the OIG, SpaceX plans to launch a storage depot, then more than 10 tanker ships, each docking with the depot and pumping in liquid oxygen and methane. The campaign would start more than 200 days before the crew launches, with a tanker every 6 days.

The audit calls the transfer of cryogenic propellant the HLS program's most significant technical challenge for SpaceX. The fluids are kept at temperatures far below freezing, and moving them between two ships in weightlessness has never been done. SpaceX did move propellant between tanks inside one Starship in March 2025. The first transfer between two vehicles was planned for March 2025 and had slipped a year, to March 2026, by the time of the audit. We found no report of it flying as of this writing.

Cadence is a second part of the problem. The plan assumes SpaceX can turn a launch pad around in 12 to 24 days, which the OIG says the company has yet to demonstrate. NASA lists the risk and tracks it. The agency's mitigation is the staged test sequence itself: transfer demonstration, then a design review, then the uncrewed lander flight.

## The heat shield

Orion returns from the moon at about 25,000 miles per hour (40,000 kilometers per hour), and the shield that protects it lost more material than predicted on the uncrewed Artemis 1 flight in 2022. NASA traced the cause and, rather than rebuild the shield, flew Artemis 2 on a modified reentry path.

That decision was tested in April. Orion splashed down off San Diego on April 10 after a 694,481-mile (1.1 million-kilometer) trip around the moon. NASA's April 20 assessment said the char loss seen on Artemis 1 "was significantly reduced, both in terms of quantity and size," and that performance matched arc jet ground tests run after Artemis 1. The agency reported "no unusual conditions identified."

This is the risk that has moved furthest toward closed. Detailed checks remain, including sample extraction and X-ray scans at Marshall Space Flight Center. NASA will use those results to confirm the material behaved as the surface inspection suggests.

## The suit

An astronaut cannot walk on the moon without a suit rated for it, and the Artemis suit is being built by a single company. Axiom Space received a 2022 contract; Collins Aerospace, the other awardee, left in 2024. In April, the OIG published report IG-26-006 on how NASA bought its suits. It looked at six comparable NASA programs and found that contract award to first flight averaged 8.7 years. Counted from the May 2022 award, that points to mid-2031.

The OIG's warning is specific: if Axiom does not deliver in time, NASA may have to change its lunar plans. The audit measures how long comparable programs took. It does not claim to know when this suit will be ready.

## Budget and politics

Artemis depends on yearly appropriations that no engineer controls. The White House asked Congress in its fiscal 2027 request for $18.8 billion for NASA overall, a 23 percent cut, but $8.5 billion of that is for Artemis, covering landers, suits, surface systems and astronaut transportation, according to SpacePolicyOnline's reading of the request. The House Appropriations Committee approved $24.438 billion for the agency, close to the fiscal 2026 level.

The OIG found that HLS contract costs have stayed stable: the SpaceX contract has grown 6 percent and Blue Origin's less than 1 percent, with NASA obligating $6.9 billion so far and estimating $18.3 billion through fiscal 2030. Fixed-price contracts shield the government from lander overruns. They do not shield the schedule, and a delayed lander still leaves a rocket and capsule waiting.

## Side by side

The risks differ in kind. The heat shield is a physical question that a flight has now largely answered. The lander and refueling risks are unflown technology, with test flights still to come. The suit is a schedule question, and the budget is a political one that Congress resolves each year.

The OIG puts the lander first because it has the highest probability of crew loss. The refueling test and the uncrewed landing demonstration are the datable events that will show how much of that risk has been retired before Artemis 4.
