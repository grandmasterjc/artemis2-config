Outside Earth's magnetic field, radiation comes straight through the walls of a spacecraft. It cannot be seen or felt. The astronauts who flew Artemis 2 around the moon in April wore small dosimeter badges to record it, and NASA has said it will publish its first interpretation of their health data later this year.

That mission was the first crewed flight beyond low Earth orbit in more than 50 years, and it raised a question many readers have asked since. What does going to the moon do to a human body, and what is NASA doing about it? The agency's Human Research Program answers with a framework of five hazards. This article takes them in turn, starting with the one that has the best data.

## The five hazards

NASA's Human Research Program groups the risks of deep-space travel as space radiation, isolation and confinement, distance from Earth, gravity fields, and hostile or closed environments. The agency calls radiation "one of the most hazardous aspects of spaceflight" on its [hazards page](https://www.nasa.gov/hrp/hazards). It also notes that the five interact and can make each other worse.

Most of what NASA knows about these hazards comes from the International Space Station (ISS), which orbits inside Earth's magnetic shield. The moon is a different case, and Artemis is the first program to gather human data there since Apollo.

## Radiation on the surface

China's Chang'e 4 lander carried the first instrument to measure the dose on the lunar surface. The German-Chinese team behind it recorded an absorbed dose rate of 13.2 microgray per hour in silicon, [according to the study in Science Advances](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.aaz1334). The team put the dose equivalent, the weighted unit that radiation limits use, at about 60 microsieverts an hour, or roughly 1,370 microsieverts a day. Thomas Berger of the German Aerospace Center (DLR) said the number is a good benchmark for the radiation inside an astronaut suit.

Compare that with the limit. NASA set a career ceiling of 600 millisieverts for every astronaut in 2022, following a recommendation from the National Academies. At the Chang'e 4 rate, a person would need about 440 days on the surface to reach it. A week-long stay adds about 10 millisieverts, by our arithmetic.

The single number hides one risk. A solar storm can deliver a comparable dose in hours.

## Inside the capsule

Orion's radiation record comes from Artemis 1, the uncrewed flight of 2022. Two mannequins named Helga and Zohar flew inside it carrying more than 6,000 passive detectors, plus 16 active ones at the positions of radiation-sensitive organs. Zohar wore an experimental protective vest. The results, [published in Nature in 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-024-07927-7), showed that dose rates differed by a factor of four between the most and least shielded spots in the cabin while Orion crossed the Van Allen belts. Turning the spacecraft 90 degrees during that crossing cut the rate by about half.

Orion's storm shelter is one of the well-shielded spots. Crews would pack stowage bags around themselves to add mass between them and the incoming particles. The DLR team calculated that a large solar particle event would deliver no more than 150 millisieverts inside the shelter. The paper's authors concluded that exposure on future Artemis flights of similar design is unlikely to exceed NASA's limits.

The vest is still being evaluated. A paper at the 2024 International Astronautical Congress estimated it would cut the dose from a storm like the one in August 1972, which fell between Apollo 16 and Apollo 17, from about 234 to about 89 millisieverts at two of the cabin's seats.

## One-sixth gravity

The moon's gravity is one-sixth of Earth's. Nobody has lived in it for longer than Apollo 17's crew, who spent about three days on the surface.

Weightlessness costs bone and muscle, and NASA's evidence report on fracture risk, led by Jean Sibonga at Johnson Space Center, says spaceflight can weaken bone strength enough to raise the risk of a break during ordinary activity. Partial gravity might be gentler. Nobody knows, because no human has been measured after weeks in it.

Artemis's countermeasure on the way there is the Orion Flywheel, selected in 2017. It gives resistance and aerobic workouts in a package small enough to stow, and it needs no power.

## Distance and confinement

Apollo missions lasted between six and 12 days. Artemis 2 flew about 10. The moon is far closer than Mars, which NASA lists at an average of 140 million miles (225 million kilometers) from Earth, and that shortens most of the hazards in the distance and isolation categories. A radio signal needs about 1.3 seconds to reach the moon, so live conversation with mission control stays possible. Medical evacuation to Earth would still take days.

For closed environments, NASA's hazard pages list temperature, pressure, lighting, noise and microbes as factors that a crew has to manage together.

## Artemis 2 data

Artemis 2 added measurements no earlier flight had. The crew took part in the Spaceflight Standard Measures study, which had only covered astronauts in low Earth orbit until now. It tracks balance, bone, muscle, heart, vision, cognition and immune function before and after flight. Their tissue was also flown in the AVATAR study, which sent chips seeded with bone marrow cells from each astronaut and let researchers compare the chips with the crew.

Results were not yet public when this article was written. NASA has said the initial report is planned for later this year, and the data will go to the agency's Life Sciences Data Archive for other scientists.
