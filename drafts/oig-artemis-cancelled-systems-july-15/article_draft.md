NASA’s Artemis campaign has been re-plotted so often that it is easy to lose track of what, exactly, has been left behind.

A new interim memorandum from NASA’s Office of Inspector General (OIG) forces that question back into focus by doing something simple: it names four major Artemis systems NASA decided to cancel or repurpose, then totals the cost and schedule posture at the moment they were effectively put on ice.

The memo is worth reading not because it breaks new ground on the current flight plan, but because it shows the size of the sunk footprint that sits under every “reformulation” slide.

And there is an additional reason it matters now: the OIG explicitly ties the decision to issue an interim memo to budget timing and near-term policy churn.

## What the OIG memo is, and why it landed now

The document is an interim memorandum, Report No. ML-26-002, addressed to Lori Glaze in her role as Acting Associate Administrator for Exploration Systems Development Mission Directorate.

The OIG explains the timing plainly: “Given the substantial investment, evolving changes to the Artemis campaign, and urgency of the current fiscal year budget cycle, we are issuing this interim memorandum to document the cost, schedule, and development posture of these systems as well as the projected cost and schedule to complete them if NASA had not reformulated the Artemis campaign.” (NASA OIG, June 24, 2026)

That sentence is doing more work than it looks like.

It is a statement that the Artemis narrative is not only a technical program story but also a budget-cycle story.

It is also a hint that the OIG expects near-term decisions about what NASA keeps funding, what it slows, and what it ends.

## The four systems the memo focuses on

The OIG says it focused on four Artemis systems “that the Agency announced plans to cancel or repurpose as part of a broader reformulation of the Artemis campaign.” (NASA OIG, June 24, 2026)

Those systems are:

- Space Launch System (SLS) Exploration Upper Stage (EUS)
- SLS Universal Stage Adapter (USA)
- Mobile Launcher 2 (ML-2)
- Gateway’s Habitation and Logistics Outpost (HALO)

Seen together, the list is revealing.

EUS and the adapter are SLS evolution hardware.

ML-2 is the ground system intended to support future SLS configurations.

HALO is not an SLS component at all, but a major element of Gateway.

The common thread is not a single contractor or a single subsystem, but a slice of Artemis that had become expensive and schedule-stretched even before it reached operations.

## The number everyone will quote: $2.8 billion to $5.9 billion

The memo’s most memorable line is a compact summary of escalation.

“Over the course of their life cycles, the combined contract values for these efforts ballooned from nearly $2.8 billion to $5.9 billion…” (NASA OIG, June 24, 2026)

In the same breath, it notes that NASA “extended their contracted delivery dates by up to 7 years.” (NASA OIG, June 24, 2026)

It is important to be careful with what that means.

Contract value is not the same as total program cost, and four line items are not the whole Artemis campaign.

But the OIG is deliberately choosing a metric that is legible to policymakers and appropriators: what NASA had committed on contract, how that changed, and how much time slipped.

If you want a simple picture of how reformulation decisions get made, this is one of the cleanest available.

## How the OIG built its projections, and what it warns readers not to over-interpret

The OIG includes unusually explicit caveats about what its projections are and are not.

“These projections are not intended to serve as comprehensive cost and schedule estimates.” (NASA OIG, June 24, 2026)

“Rather, they illustrate potential outcomes based on available historical cost and schedule data provided by NASA.” (NASA OIG, June 24, 2026)

For each system, the OIG says it evaluated “cost, schedule, and development posture as well as the estimated cost and schedule at completion if it had continued at similar performance levels.” (NASA OIG, June 24, 2026)

On schedule analysis, the memo notes: “We did not perform electronic testing or validation of the schedule files.” (NASA OIG, June 24, 2026)

And on cost forecasting, it flags that “each projection required unique assumptions to account for differences in contract structures, procurement histories, and data availability.” (NASA OIG, June 24, 2026)

In other words: the point is not to publish a single definitive number that can be used as a talking point for the next decade.

The point is to show the direction and order of magnitude of what tends to happen when complex NASA development programs begin missing their original plans.

## Why cancelled systems still affect the Artemis story you track today

It can be tempting to treat a cancelled or repurposed system as a historical curiosity: a box crossed out on an architecture chart.

The OIG memo suggests a more practical view.

First, the money and workforce involved are real.

Even if a system is stopped, the agency has already spent years of contractor effort, internal engineering time, and facility bandwidth.

Second, cancellation decisions are not isolated.

Removing EUS or ML-2 is not just a technical scope change; it forces NASA to re-state what future SLS flights look like, and what ground infrastructure is truly needed.

Third, a Gateway element appearing on the same list as SLS hardware underlines that Artemis reformulation is cutting across centers and directorates.

If you were hoping the program would be stabilized by simply choosing between lunar landers, the HALO inclusion is a reminder that the architecture question is broader.

That is why a memo about cancelled systems can still be a present-tense Artemis document.

It describes constraints the program is living with now, not only the hardware it may never fly.

## A note on what this memo does not say

Because the memo is narrowly scoped, it does not answer several questions readers will naturally ask.

It does not assign blame to a single contractor or a single NASA decision.

It does not provide a complete Artemis cost picture.

And it does not, on its own, tell you what NASA will fund next.

But it does show how quickly four “non-flying” lines of hardware can become large enough that the watchdog decides to issue a time-sensitive interim memo.

That is, arguably, the headline.

## Where this leaves us, and what to watch next

The most actionable way to read ML-26-002 is as a baseline for the next round of Artemis budgeting and re-planning.

If NASA proposes another architecture adjustment, watch for:

- Whether the agency explicitly accounts for sunk contract value and schedule posture when it retires elements
- Whether Congress asks for the same kind of cost and schedule accounting on other Artemis work in progress
- Whether Gateway’s role is clarified, narrowed, or re-sequenced, given that HALO is included in the cancelled-or-repurposed list

The OIG is still clear that this is interim work: “We have not yet completed the audit.” (NASA OIG, June 24, 2026)

A final audit report could add more detail on what happened inside each program line.

For now, the interim memo accomplishes something rare in Artemis coverage: it makes the cost of changing the plan concrete.
