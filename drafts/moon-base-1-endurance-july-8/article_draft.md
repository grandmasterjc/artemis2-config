On May 26, NASA named its first Moon Base landing. Moon Base I — described as the first privately funded lunar lander mission in history — would send a robotic Blue Origin lander called Endurance to the Shackleton Connecting Ridge near the lunar south pole, carrying NASA's first surface payloads, and it was targeted to launch as soon as this fall. (NASA, May 26, 2026)

Two days later, on May 28, the rocket meant to fly it exploded. Blue Origin's New Glenn was destroyed during a prelaunch static fire test at Launch Complex 36A at Cape Canaveral, and the pad itself was severely damaged — including the transporter-erector that raises the rocket vertical. Launch operations were suspended pending investigation and repairs. (May 28, 2026)

The result is an unusual split. The lander is passing its tests while the launch site that would send it to the Moon has to be rebuilt from damaged hardware. Endurance is close to ready. The pad is not.

That mismatch — spacecraft ready, launch site not — is quietly reshaping the near-term Moon Base timeline. The Mark 1 lander is now expected to fly in the first quarter of 2027, a slip from the fall-2026 target NASA announced just before the accident. (SatNews, July 6, 2026)

## What Endurance is supposed to do

Endurance is the uncrewed version of Blue Origin's Blue Moon Mark 1 lander, and its job on Moon Base I is to make the first crewed landing less dangerous. It will attempt a precision touchdown on the Shackleton Connecting Ridge, demonstrate cryogenic propulsion and autonomous guidance, navigation, and control, and deliver NASA's payloads to the surface. (NASA)

Those payloads will look familiar to anyone who read the July 1 briefing. Moon Base I carries the same three-instrument bundle NASA is standardizing across its early landers:

- SCALPSS, a set of four cameras that build a 3D view of how an engine's exhaust plume kicks up lunar dust during descent
- LRA, a passive laser retroreflector that lets orbiters and landers fix a precise location
- LETS, a spectrometer that measures the surface radiation environment

Flying identical instruments on lander after lander is the whole point: it turns each landing into a comparable data point rather than a one-off. Moon Base I is meant to be the first of those data points, and the science is explicitly framed as risk reduction for the crewed Artemis landing now scheduled for 2028. (NASA)

## The pad that isn't ready

The problem is not the lander. It is the ride.

New Glenn exploded at LC-36A on May 28, and the damage went beyond the vehicle. Blue Origin is not simply replacing what was destroyed; it is rebuilding the pad and accelerating a previously planned redesign of the heavily damaged transporter-erector, destacking the main tower and working steps in parallel to move faster. The company is targeting a New Glenn return to flight by the end of 2026 — a timeline outside observers have called very aggressive — and is speeding up a second pad, LC-36B, meant to enter service by late 2027. (SpaceNews; Ars Technica, June 2026)

Only after New Glenn is flying again can Endurance follow. That is why a lander that is nearly finished is still waiting: its schedule is now set by concrete and steel at Cape Canaveral, not by anything happening on the spacecraft itself. That return-to-flight date is the milestone the whole south-pole schedule now hangs on, and it is exactly the kind of launch our companion app, Liftoff, is built to track.

## Why the lander keeps moving anyway

The encouraging part, reported this week, is that the two schedules have decoupled. The Mark 1 program is under manifest pressure from the pad rebuild, but the lander assembly lines run independently of the launch-facility cleanup, so risk-reduction work is continuing on schedule — cryogenic loading simulations and BE-7 lander-engine test firings among them — so the spacecraft are flight-qualified by the time the rebuilt transporter-erector is back. (SatNews, July 6, 2026; SpaceNews)

That tracks with where Endurance already was. The Blue Moon Mark 1 completed a campaign in NASA's large thermal-vacuum chamber this spring, one of the last major environmental hurdles before a lander is cleared to fly. In other words, the hardware kept advancing through the exact weeks the pad was being cleared of wreckage.

## What the slip actually costs

A few months on a robotic pathfinder is not, by itself, a crisis. But it lands on a program that has been selling cadence — order missions faster, launch more often, learn by repetition. That bet only works if launch sites are available, and right now one of the two rockets meant to open the Moon Base era is grounded until it can prove itself again.

The near-term consequence is weight shifting onto what still flies. Until New Glenn returns, NASA's south-pole ramp-up leans harder on SpaceX and on the landers that already have a working ride. NASA administrator Jared Isaacman, who inspected the damaged pad by helicopter, told the agency's workforce that the incident could potentially affect NASA's Artemis and Moon Base plans. None of that changes the destination. It changes the pace, and pace is the entire premise of the Moon Base approach.

## Where this leaves us

The next milestones are unusually concrete. Watch for New Glenn's return to flight, targeted for late 2026; then Moon Base I itself, now expected in the first quarter of 2027; and finally Endurance's precision landing at the Shackleton Connecting Ridge, the first real test of whether these payloads can do their risk-reduction job before crews arrive.
