Orion was farther from Earth than any people had ever been.

It was April 6, the sixth day of Artemis 2, and the capsule was swinging around the far side of the moon. In the cabin, commander Reid Wiseman was watching a counter.

Houston had told him the farthest the crew was supposed to get: 219,667 nautical miles (406,823 kilometers). The counter reached it. "I thought, 'Great, this is the farthest we will be from Earth, and the next number I will see will be 219,666,'" Wiseman later told an audience at the Starmus VIII festival in Tenerife on Oct. 17, according to Live Science.

But the next number was 219,668.

Three minutes later it read 219,669. "That was the moment that I felt real fear because I thought we had just gone too far," Wiseman said. Then the number began to fall, and the crew cheered.

What was the counter measuring, was there really a point of no return, and how does a capsule that coasts around the moon get back? Live Science's report of the talk, NASA's flight descriptions and the Canadian Space Agency (CSA) mission pages answer most of it.

## The counter's numbers

Houston's figure was in nautical miles because that is how Wiseman read it. Live Science converted it to 252,788 statute miles, or 406,823 kilometers. One nautical mile is 1.852 kilometers, so the two extra ticks he saw amount to about 3.7 kilometers. Against a distance of more than 400,000 kilometers, the miss was roughly one part in 100,000.

NASA's own count differs a little. Its April 6 release said Orion would reach about 252,756 miles (406,771 kilometers) from Earth, and other outlets quoted figures within a few miles of that during the flight. The planning figure Wiseman quoted is about 30 miles higher. None of the published accounts says why, and this article can't settle it either.

## Free-return path

The phrase "turn-back point" suggests a decision. The Artemis 2 trajectory had none. About 25 hours after launch, after a day in high Earth orbit, Orion's service module fired for 6 minutes and 5 seconds. That translunar injection burn put the capsule on what the CSA calls a free return trajectory. The combined gravity of Earth and the moon carries the spacecraft around the far side, and Earth's gravity pulls it back without an engine burn to turn around.

Houston's 219,667 was therefore a forecast of where the curve would peak, not a boundary the crew could cross. Gravity bends a path like this, and its farthest point shifts slightly with small differences in the burn and in the moon's pull. The flyby also took Orion behind the moon, out of radio contact with Earth for about 40 minutes, NASA said.

## Slow change at the peak

Near the far end of the loop, Orion is moving mostly sideways relative to Earth, so its distance changes little from minute to minute. That fits Wiseman's account of one nautical mile every three minutes. At the top of the curve the number stops rising and starts to fall. For a crew watching it, there is a stretch where nothing seems decided.

The peak depends on where the moon is. Its distance from Earth varies over each month from about 363,300 kilometers to about 405,500 kilometers, Scientific American reported. Artemis 2 passed 6,544 kilometers above the lunar surface, the CSA said, and the capsule's farthest point from Earth lay beyond the moon.

## Apollo 13's route

The previous record was 248,655 miles (400,171 kilometers), set by Apollo 13 on April 15, 1970. According to collectSPACE, that crew used a free-return path because of doubts about the spacecraft's main engine after an oxygen tank failed, and began falling back soon after passing the mark. Artemis 2 passed the same distance on April 6 and kept going, to a peak about 4,100 miles (6,600 kilometers) farther out. Orion on Artemis 1, flying without a crew in 2022, went farther still: 268,563 miles (432,210 kilometers), collectSPACE reported.

Artemis 2 planned the free-return path from the start. That saved propellant for tests of Orion's systems, collectSPACE reported, so the record came with the route. It was not the purpose.

Mission control marked the passing with a message from Jim Lovell, the Apollo 13 commander, who died in August 2025. He had recorded it before his death, and it opened with "Welcome to my old neighborhood." Hansen said the crew hoped the new mark would not stand for long.

## Work at the window

Wiseman said no one noticed his fear. Victor Glover and Christina Koch were at the window making science observations of the moon, and Jeremy Hansen was sending reports to mission control. In an interview with The Week Junior Science+Nature, Wiseman also credited the flight control teams on the ground for the safe return and called them the true heroes of the mission.

When the counter dropped back to 219,668, Wiseman told the audience, he said, "We're going home." He added that he knew there was a good chance they would make it. The rest of the trip went as planned. Orion left the moon's sphere of influence on flight day 7 and reentered the atmosphere on April 10 at 35 times the speed of sound, ending a flight of 9 days, 1 hour and 32 minutes that covered more than 1.2 million kilometers, according to the CSA.
