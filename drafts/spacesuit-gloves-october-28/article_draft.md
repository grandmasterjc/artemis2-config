Pull a rubber glove over your hand, seal the cuff and pump it up until it is firm. Now close your fist. The glove fights you the whole way, because the air inside has to go somewhere and the walls want to stay round. Every spacewalker does a version of that for hours at a time, usually while turning a bolt or holding a tool.

The glove is the part of a spacesuit that astronauts complain about most, and it is the part that decides whether a person can actually work outside. What makes it so hard to build, what does it do to the hands inside it, and what changes when the work moves from orbit to the lunar surface? This article draws on NASA technical papers, a 2010 study from the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT) and NASA's own cold-chamber tests of its current glove.

## A balloon around the hand

A suit has to hold in air. NASA's current spacewalking suit, the Extravehicular Mobility Unit (EMU), runs at about 4.3 pounds per square inch (30 kilopascals). That is less than a third of the pressure at sea level, and it sounds gentle. It is enough to make a sealed glove balloon outward, and NASA's gloves are built in layers to cope with that: a pressure bladder, a restraint layer that stops the bladder from expanding, and an outer thermal and micrometeoroid garment.

Each layer adds stiffness. A pressurized glove wants to hold one shape, which is roughly a mitten gripping a small cylinder. Moving away from that shape takes effort, and a 1997 NASA technical paper found that the heaviest load lands on the metacarpophalangeal (MCP) joint, the knuckle where each finger meets the palm. Gripping a tool means bending those joints against the suit's own pressure, again and again.

## Apollo's verdict

The Apollo suits ran at a lower pressure than the EMU, and the crews still had trouble. In 1993, seven surviving moonwalkers took part in a NASA review of their surface work. As summarized in The Space Review by space historian Dave Day, all of those who discussed gloves called their own "barely adequate." Their top recommendation was "Better gloves, better gloves, better gloves." They said the gloves severely limited mobility and dexterity, and that arm fatigue set in within minutes of starting a moonwalk.

The same group thought low operating pressure and a pure oxygen atmosphere were the main keys to a flexible suit. Custom-fitted gloves, they said, were a necessity. NASA now makes them for every astronaut who trains for spacewalks.

## Fingernails and fatigue

Gloves leave marks on the people wearing them. In 2010, Dava Newman, Roedolph Opperman and Alan Natapoff of MIT published an analysis of Johnson Space Center injury records for 232 crew members in the journal Aviation, Space, and Environmental Medicine. They focused on fingernail delamination, in which a nail detaches from the nail bed. Crew members whose knuckle circumference exceeded 22.8 centimeters (about 9 inches) were roughly four times as likely to be hurt: about 20 percent against 5 percent for smaller hands.

The authors proposed a mechanism. Because glove design limits how far the MCP joint moves, the fingertips are pressed against the glove while the hand works, which cuts blood flow and damages tissue. A 2005 study cited by MIT found that nearly half of 350 spacewalk-training injuries reported from 2002 to 2004 involved the hands. A 2015 NASA Johnson Space Center report lists finger delamination, joint pain and redness among the injuries astronauts report from the suit.

## Gloves since Shuttle

The glove on today's EMU is called Phase VI, the sixth version of a design NASA has used since the 1980s. In the 2015 NASA analysis, preliminary sensor results showed it placed less strain and shear on the hand than the earlier Series 4000 glove. The same report states the central trade-off: a glove that transmits more feel to the astronaut also transmits more force to the hand.

NASA has also tried outside help. Its Astronaut Glove Challenge, run in 2007 and 2009 as part of the Centennial Challenges prize program, rewarded designs that bent easily and still held pressure. Peter Homer, an engineer from Maine, won both times, taking $250,000 in 2009. In the burst test that year, runner-up Ted Southern's glove began leaking at 17 pounds per square inch, while Homer's held to the 20 pounds per square inch maximum the rig could supply, according to news coverage of the event.

## Cold and the south pole

A glove for the moon has a second job that a station glove never needs to do, which is touching ground and tools at temperatures far below anything in orbit. NASA's Engineering and Safety Center (NESC) ran a glove test campaign from October 2023 to March 2024 in a chamber called CITADEL at the Jet Propulsion Laboratory in California. Engineers put a Phase VI glove on a manikin hand with fluid loops that simulate warm blood and dozens of temperature sensors, then cooled it in vacuum to minus 352 degrees Fahrenheit (minus 213 degrees Celsius).

The result, as reported by JPL, was that the current glove did not meet thermal requirements for the south pole. Permanently shadowed regions there can fall as low as minus 414 degrees Fahrenheit (minus 248 degrees Celsius), and JPL said astronauts might spend up to two hours at a time in them. NASA engineer Zach Fester told JPL that gloves and boots matter because they make prolonged contact with cold surfaces and tools. The tests were meant to help set repeatable methods for qualifying the next suit, the one Axiom Space is building.

## Axiom's glove

Axiom Space describes the glove on its Axiom Extravehicular Mobility Unit (AxEMU) as custom-made and built in-house, designed so astronauts can handle specialized tools for exploration and science. Gloves are also one of the few parts of the suit that have to be sized to the individual wearer, while most other components adjust to fit a range of body types.

Public detail on the glove itself is thin. Aerospace America reported in May 2026 that Axiom was preparing a qualification campaign of about a year, ending in mid-2027 if the schedule holds. No glove-specific results have been published.

Back to the balloon. A moon glove still has to hold air. It also has to let a hand close around a rock hammer, hour after hour, in a place colder than anything the legacy glove was tested for.
