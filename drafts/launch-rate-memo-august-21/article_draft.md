President Trump signed a national security presidential memorandum on Thursday titled "The National Space Transportation Policy," the first rewrite of that policy since 2013. It hands deadlines to half a dozen departments, and it puts one number above everything else in the document. "By 2030, our space transportation ranges must grow to support more than 1,000 launches and reentries every year," it reads.

Much of the coverage that followed treated the memorandum as an order to return Americans to the moon by 2028. It contains no such instruction. The text does not mention 2028, and it sets no date for a crewed landing. That goal is real, but it was signed eight months earlier, in Executive Order 14369, "Ensuring American Space Superiority," dated December 18, 2025.

Thursday's document aims at something narrower and, for Artemis, more load-bearing: how often the United States can fly at all. That makes the 1,000 figure worth testing, and the most direct test comes from the agency that licenses these operations. In its Aerospace Forecast for fiscal years 2026 through 2046, the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) projects commercial launch and reentry operations rising from 214 in fiscal 2026 to 507 in fiscal 2036 under its high-case scenario. That is roughly half the White House figure, arriving six years after the deadline.

## What the memorandum directs agencies to do

The new policy instructs the government to "favor commercial space transportation services for meeting United States Government needs" and to see that federal payloads fly on vehicles built in the United States.

Most of its substance is deadlines. The Secretary of the Interior has 90 days to identify federal land that could host a reentry site. The Departments of State and Commerce have 120 days to update export policies. Within 180 days, the Secretary of War and the NASA administrator are to develop scheduling criteria for federal ranges so commercial users can move through them more efficiently, and the Secretary of Transportation is to identify locations for additional launch facilities and fold launch operations into airspace modernization.

The lunar surface appears once in an operational sense. Section 6(c) directs the NASA administrator to "develop a lunar logistics architecture that facilitates commercial transportation to and from the lunar surface." That is an instruction about cargo rather than crew, and it sits beside parallel language on robotic and eventually human access to Mars.

## Where the 2028 landing goal actually comes from

Executive Order 14369 is the document that set the lunar dates. It called for astronauts on the moon by 2028, the initial elements of a permanent lunar outpost by 2030, and nuclear reactors in space by the end of the decade.

NASA's own plan arrives at those dates by a different route. On February 27 this year, Administrator Jared Isaacman restructured Artemis 3, removing the landing and turning the flight into a crewed rendezvous and docking test in low Earth orbit. The first crewed landing moved to Artemis 4, which the agency is targeting for 2028. Asked at Kennedy Space Center on August 14 whether Artemis 3 would fly next year, Isaacman said he was "extremely confident" that it would, though that mission as it now stands does not reach the moon.

## What the licensing agency expects

The FAA's numbers give the target a baseline. In fiscal 2025, licensed launch and reentry operations reached 204, which the agency calls the highest annual total in United States history. That figure was 195 launches and nine reentries, and it came from a short list of places. Cape Canaveral accounted for 76 launches and Vandenberg Space Force Base for 61, with another 26 from Kennedy Space Center.

Growth has been steep by any measure. Of the 982 launches and reentries the FAA licensed between 1989 and 2025, 603 of them, about 61 percent, came in the five years from 2021 through 2025. Even against that curve, the agency's high-case scenario tops out at 507 operations in fiscal 2036, and its low case reaches only 282.

One distinction matters here, and it cuts both ways. The memorandum's target is capacity, what ranges "must grow to support," while the FAA forecasts demand, the flights operators expect to fly. A range can be built for more traffic than turns up. Ranges are still sized against expected demand, though, and the FAA moved the other way this year. Its high-case scenario for fiscal 2026 through 2034 came down by 545 operations from last year's forecast, a reduction the agency attributes to slower Mars plans, one launch provider shifting toward missile defense work and a pause in Blue Origin's New Shepard flights until at least 2028.

## Why the launch rate decides the moon schedule

Artemis is unusually sensitive to this number. As restructured, Artemis 3 needs three launches on three different rockets, with Orion meeting a Blue Moon test article and a Starship in low Earth orbit. The landing on Artemis 4 depends on Starship's Human Landing System (HLS), which cannot reach the moon on a single launch. It has to be fueled in orbit by a series of tanker flights.

How many is contested. The Government Accountability Office (GAO) has put the figure at 16 launches for one HLS mission. NASA estimates have ranged from 16 to 19. SpaceX has been more optimistic, with a company vice president saying in 2024 that the number would be "10-ish," which SpaceX has said is subject to change.

Set even the low end against fiscal 2025's 195 licensed launches and the scale becomes visible. A single lunar landing could absorb somewhere between 5 and 8 percent of a record American launch year, on top of everything else trying to fly.

That arithmetic is why a transportation policy, rather than a landing proclamation, is the kind of document that can move an Artemis date. Cadence is the constraint the program keeps running into, and it is the constraint Thursday's memorandum is written against.

## What to watch

The memorandum's deadlines produce checkable dates. Identification of a federal reentry site falls due around November 18, and the export policy updates around December 18. The range scheduling criteria and the list of additional launch site locations are both due around February 16, 2027. NASA's lunar logistics architecture under Section 6(c) carries no public deadline, so the thing to watch there is whether the agency describes what it means by it.

Starship Flight 14, expected late this month, is the nearest hard test of cadence, since it would be the vehicle's first attempt to reach orbit and its first attempt at catching the ship with the launch tower. What follows it matters as much, because Isaacman has said he wants SpaceX launching Starship every couple of weeks before the end of the year. The FAA's next annual forecast will show whether the agency thinks any of this changes its numbers.

Thursday's memorandum is a real policy document with real deadlines attached to named officials, and the problem it picks is the right one. American launch capacity, rather than presidential enthusiasm, governs how quickly hardware can be assembled in orbit, and Artemis now depends on assembling hardware in orbit twice before anyone stands on the moon.

The distance between the target and the forecast is still there. The White House has asked for ranges able to handle more than 1,000 launches and reentries a year within four years. The agency that licenses those flights does not expect the country to reach half that number until the middle of the next decade, and it lowered its own projection this year rather than raising it.
