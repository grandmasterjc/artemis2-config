SpaceX has pushed back Starship's 14th test flight twice this month, most recently from Sept. 22 to no earlier than Sept. 28. The company gave one reason for the latest slip: it is still waiting on the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) to finish reviewing the flight, [Space.com reported](https://www.space.com/space-exploration/launches-spacecraft/spacex-next-starship-launch-now-on-sept-28-for-1st-orbital-flight). Nothing about the hardware changed. A Super Heavy booster that had already fired all 33 of its engines on the pad, and a Starship upper stage integrated and ready to fly, sat waiting on a piece of paperwork.

That delay reaches beyond the six days it costs SpaceX. In August, NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman told reporters at Kennedy Space Center that his confidence in an on-time Artemis 3 rested on this specific flight. "If they're able to launch again in early September after their last launch and continue to track inside the month, potentially every couple of weeks before the end of the year, we're going to feel very good about their time to contribute to Artemis 3 next year," he said, according to [Spaceflight Now](https://spaceflightnow.com/2026/08/14/nasa-administrator-extremely-confident-in-artemis-3-launch-in-2027/). SpaceX's last launch before that remark was Flight 13, on July 25. Flight 14 was supposed to be the one that kept the cadence going. It is now targeting a date more than three weeks past the window Isaacman described, contingent on a license the agency hasn't issued yet.

## A cadence with a deadline

Isaacman's Aug. 14 remarks were not a vague expression of optimism. He described a specific test: an early-September launch, followed by a flight roughly every two weeks through the end of the year. He was standing next to real progress when he said it. Solid rocket booster segments for the Space Launch System (SLS) were stacking in the Vehicle Assembly Building, and Orion's crew and service modules had been joined on July 30. Starship was the piece he couldn't point to directly. It was still cutting hardware for its next test vehicle, and its flight cadence was the one variable he framed as a condition rather than a certainty.

## A slipping target

That condition has not been met. A Federal Communications Commission (FCC) filing on Sept. 2 listed Sept. 15 as SpaceX's planned deployment date for the mission's satellite payload, according to [Tesla Oracle](https://www.teslaoracle.com/2026/09/02/fcc-filing-reveals-starship-flight-14-launch-on-september-15-spacex-conducts-33-engine-static-fire-on-booster-21/). That date slipped to Sept. 22, and then slipped again, to Sept. 28, with SpaceX citing an FAA review that had not concluded. Unlike the 13 Starship test flights that came before it, Flight 14 is attempting to reach a sustained Earth orbit rather than the deliberately suborbital arc every earlier flight flew. That distinction is why it needs a different kind of clearance: regulators require a fuller review before authorizing a vehicle to circle the planet for the first time, given the debris risk if something goes wrong once it's up there. SpaceX has not said when it expects that review to close, only that Sept. 28 remains the target "pending regulatory approval."

## The pathfinder's real requirement

Artemis 3 actually needs less from Starship than people often assume. The mission is no longer the flight that lands astronauts on the moon; NASA reassigned that job to Artemis 4 in February, when it restructured the campaign. What Artemis 3 requires is a single Starship, described in [NASA's own mission outline](https://www.nasa.gov/missions/artemis/artemis-3/nasa-outlines-preliminary-artemis-iii-mission-plans/) as a human landing system "pathfinder," fitted with a docking system and flown to Earth orbit to rendezvous with Orion. Astronauts won't enter it. No ten-tanker refueling campaign, no trans-lunar injection, no landing attempt is bundled into this particular test; that full sequence still lies ahead of Artemis 4. What Flight 14 actually tests is narrower, and in its way more basic: whether SpaceX can build and fly Starship V3 vehicles reliably enough, and often enough, to deliver that one pathfinder on schedule. Isaacman's cadence bar was a proxy for exactly that question, not for the harder problem still waiting further down the line.

## Still on the pad

Flight 14 carries 26 Starlink V3 satellites, each about 2,000 kilograms (4,400 pounds), roughly three times the mass of the older V2 Mini model, with three of them fitted with cameras to inspect Starship's heat shield in flight, [according to reporting on the mission](https://www.thenews.com.pk/latest/1417179-starship-flight-14-why-spacexs-first-ever-orbital-test-matters-to-nasas-artemis). If it launches, the plan calls for six orbits over roughly 10 hours before a controlled splashdown in the Pacific Ocean. SpaceX has also scaled back its ambitions for the attempt. An earlier plan to catch the returning Super Heavy booster back at the launch tower was dropped in favor of a controlled splashdown in the Gulf of Mexico, [SpaceNews reported](https://spacenews.com/spacex-scales-back-plans-for-next-starship-launch/) in August. Booster 21 completed a 33-engine static fire on Aug. 28 and rolled out to the pad on Sept. 20. Everything SpaceX can control on the ground is done. What remains isn't.

## The next six weeks

Whether Isaacman's bar gets cleared will show up in dates, not statements. The first is Sept. 28 itself, still conditional on the FAA license SpaceX is waiting for. The second is what happens after that, assuming the flight goes up: Isaacman's own standard was a launch roughly every two weeks through December, which would put the next Starship flight in mid-October if SpaceX is going to match the pace he described. NASA's public updates on Artemis 3 processing, which have run roughly monthly since SLS stacking began, are where that cadence would get confirmed, or quietly dropped without comment.

Five weeks ago, Isaacman picked a specific, checkable test for the one part of Artemis 3 he couldn't yet vouch for: an early-September Starship launch, followed by a flight every couple of weeks after it. The calendar has already passed the date he named. Flight 14 is still on the pad in Texas, waiting on a federal license, and whatever cadence follows it will show up in NASA's own processing updates over the coming weeks, not in anything said back in August.
