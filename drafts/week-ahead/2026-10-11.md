# The Week Ahead: October 12 to 18, 2026

A short briefing on what to watch in human lunar exploration and the surrounding launch market over the next seven days.

---

## A thin manifest, with one launch that matters

Only one flight this week carries NASA's name. Times are UTC, and where sources disagree, the disagreement is stated.

- Falcon 9 / Starlink Group 15-25: Monday, October 12, window opening at 23:00 UTC (4 p.m. PDT), from Vandenberg SLC-4E, with 27 satellites on a booster flying for the 20th time, according to [Spaceflight Now](https://spaceflightnow.com/launch-schedule/). Last week's edition listed this mission for October 10, so it has already slipped.
- Falcon 9 / CRS-35: Tuesday, October 13, at 10:33 UTC (6:33 a.m. EDT), from Cape Canaveral SLC-40, with a backup opportunity on October 14 at about 10:08 UTC. Both [SpaceX's mission page](https://www.spacex.com/launches/crs35) and Spaceflight Now give 10:33. The Wikipedia launch list shows 11:30 UTC, which I treat as stale. Dragon takes more than a day to reach the station, so docking would follow later in the week.
- Falcon 9 / USSF-481: Thursday, October 15, around 23:44 UTC, from Vandenberg. Only the [Wikipedia launch list](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_spaceflight_launches_in_October%E2%80%93December_2026) carries this date, and the payload is classified. Treat it as unconfirmed.

Just outside the window, JAXA's H3 is listed for October 19 at about 19:41 UTC from Tanegashima, carrying the Martian Moons eXploration probe. I found no confirmed Chinese, Rocket Lab or Ariane launches inside the week.

## What CRS-35 is carrying

One secondary [report](https://teslanorth.com/2026/10/07/spacex-crs-35-cargo-dragon-october-13/) says the cargo includes the ISS's final roll-out solar arrays. I could not confirm that against a NASA page. NASA's launch coverage is due to begin at about 6:15 a.m. EDT.

The station crew is smaller than a week ago. Crew-12 undocked on October 7 and its Dragon, Freedom, splashed down off California at 11:34 a.m. EDT on October 8, per [NASA's station blog](https://www.nasa.gov/blogs/spacestation/2026/10/08/nasas-spacex-crew-12-dragon-splashes-down-at-1134-a-m-edt/). Last week's edition said the undocking time was unconfirmed. Jessica Meir, Jack Hathaway, Sophie Adenot and Andrey Fedyaev spent 237 days in space.

## Artemis II's science archive is now public

On October 7, NASA released the complete lunar science dataset from Artemis II, which it describes as the first planetary science dataset captured primarily by humans in more than half a century ([NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/blogs/missions/2026/10/07/nasa-releases-artemis-ii-lunar-science-data-images/)). The release covers more than 800 GB in the Planetary Data System, over 11,000 full-resolution images and videos, and 8.5 hours of audio in which the crew describes what they saw during the April 6 lunar flyby.

One detail stands out. Reid Wiseman, Victor Glover and Jeremy Hansen each marked impact flashes they observed on the lunar surface, in blue, green and red on an annotated tablet image. NASA also published a preliminary science report, an operations report and a user guide, and says the data is already informing planning for Artemis III and later surface work.

## Starship Flight 15 has moved toward November

Starship reached orbit on September 28, but the next flight looks further off than it did. Wikipedia's Flight 15 page lists a no-earlier-than date of November 8, sourced to an air traffic planning document retrieved on October 9. That replaces the October 19 date from provisional planning that circulated after Flight 14. Neither date is a SpaceX announcement, so both should be read as planning figures.

What makes Flight 15 important for the Moon is what it might attempt. Elon Musk said in September that a successful Flight 14 could clear the way for a first catch of the ship at the tower, putting the odds at "at least 50 or 60 percent," and Ship 42 completed catch-related pad tests on September 11 and 12. SpaceX has not reconfirmed the recovery plan, and I found no FAA licence news.

## The acceleration update is still missing, and Blue Moon is far off

Jared Isaacman said at Payload's Off World conference on September 24 that an Artemis acceleration update would arrive in early October. As of Sunday I found no such release from NASA. The themes he named earlier are a Moon base, south pole landers on a monthly cadence from 2027, and nuclear power and propulsion. Until a document appears, those remain stated ambitions without schedules attached.

Blue Origin's timeline is longer. Executive John Couluris said the first Blue Moon Mark 1 launch is expected in the first quarter of 2027, and it depends on New Glenn returning to flight after the May 28 static-fire explosion at Launch Complex 36. Spaceflight Now reported in August that Blue Origin had [identified an engine issue](https://spaceflightnow.com/2026/08/06/blue-origin-identifies-engine-issue-behind-new-glenn-explosion/) behind it.

## Dates that could still move

CRS-35 has a one-day backup. USSF-481 appears in only one source. Flight 15 sits on a planning date SpaceX has not confirmed, and the Artemis acceleration document has no date beyond "early October," which has now passed.
