# The Week Ahead — June 29–July 5, 2026

A short briefing on what to watch in human lunar exploration and the surrounding launch market over the next seven days.

---

## What's actually launching

The manifest thickens a bit this week, with one mission that matters for the broader lunar-economy picture.

- Falcon 9 / SXM-11 — June 29, 02:25 UTC from Cape Canaveral SLC-40. SiriusXM radio satellite.
- South Korean ADD SLV demo flight — June 29, 05:00 UTC. First flight of the Agency for Defense Development's small launch vehicle.
- Electron / iQPS-7 — June 30 from Mahia, New Zealand.
- Pegasus-XL / Swift Boost — June 30, 10:23 UTC. Slipped a few days from last week's target. The Katalyst LINK servicing demonstration mission, attempting to attach to and reboost NASA's Neil Gehrels Swift Observatory.
- Long March 4C — July 1, 23:45 UTC from China.
- Falcon 9 / Starlink 17-46 — July 2, 02:00 UTC from Vandenberg.
- Atlas V 551 / Amazon Leo LA-08 — July 2, 04:24 UTC from Cape Canaveral. The penultimate Atlas V flight, carrying Amazon's Project Kuiper broadband satellites.
- Falcon 9 / Starlink 10-50 — July 3, 11:20 UTC.

The Atlas V is the one to note. ULA has two flights left on the manifest after this, and the vehicle that flew Curiosity, Perseverance, and a generation of national-security payloads is winding down. Amazon Kuiper is one of the rare commercial customers left for it.

## Artemis III: stacking week begins

This is the week the long-awaited integration phase actually starts.

NASA confirmed in mid-June that SLS booster stacking for Artemis III will begin in July inside Vehicle Assembly Building high bay 3 at Kennedy ([ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/06/260611024606.htm)). All ten booster segments — including the unique spacer that replaces what would normally be an interim cryogenic propulsion stage — are now at KSC. Exploration Ground Systems teams have been performing fit checks and preparing mobile launcher 1 to receive hardware.

The core stage weather cover arrived at KSC last week aboard the Pegasus barge, completing the supporting infrastructure NASA needs before vertical integration ([Friends of NASA](https://www.friendsofnasa.org/2026/06/nasa-artemis-iii-sls-core-stage-weather.html)). The cover protects the core stage's exposed top during operations between core stage mating and upper-segment installation — a Florida-weather hedge that wasn't part of the Artemis I or II flows in the same form.

What to watch this week:
- Whether NASA publishes any imagery of the first booster aft segments being lifted onto MLP-1. The stacking sequence proceeds segment-by-segment over several weeks.
- Confirmation that the spacer segment — the unique piece replacing the ICPS for Artemis III — has finished fabrication. Program manager Jeremy Parsons said in early June that work was underway, with no completion date published.
- Engine installation progress on the core stage. The first RS-25 was unboxed in the VAB on June 16; the remaining three were expected to arrive over the following weeks.

NASA still describes Artemis III's launch date as "no earlier than mid-2027," and that depends on a chain of milestones being hit: stacking, tanking test at Pad 39B by year-end, Starship HLS propellant-transfer demonstrations, and human-rating of the lander. Stacking is the first of those that produces visible hardware on the pad.

## Starship: Ship 40 single-engine done, Booster 20 slipping

SpaceX completed a full-duration single-engine static fire of Ship 40 at Massey's Test Facility on the night of June 25–26 ([SpaceX on X via Reddit](https://www.reddit.com/r/spacex/comments/1ugadxr/spacex_on_x_full_duration_singleengine_static/)). It was the vehicle's first hot-fire test, and the run completed nominally with no apparent abort.

The next step for Ship 40 is multi-engine testing — three engines, then full six-engine — in the coming weeks. That sequence has historically taken three to four weeks for previous Starship vehicles.

Booster 20 is the story that changed. SpaceX had been targeting an early-July static fire of the booster's 33 Raptor 3 engines. That has now slipped to "at least a couple of weeks" away, with the pad infrastructure cited as the limiting factor ([NextBigFuture](https://www.nextbigfuture.com/2026/06/spacex-starship-40-progressing-to-a-static-fire.html)). The Orbital Launch Mount needs additional work before it can host a full-stack hold-down test.

Practical effect: Flight 13, which had been targeting July 3, will not happen this week. A revised target is likely to land in mid- to late July. The Artemis-relevant milestone — propellant transfer between two orbiting Starships — is still on the manifest for Flight 14 or 15, no earlier than autumn.

## OIG memo: $5.9B in canceled hardware

NASA's Office of Inspector General published an interim memorandum on June 24 with a number that frames every Artemis budget conversation for the rest of the year: $5.9 billion spent on Artemis hardware that has been canceled or will not fly ([Space.com](https://www.space.com/space-exploration/artemis/nasa-spent-usd5-9-billion-on-artemis-program-hardware-it-may-never-use), [OIG memo PDF](https://oig.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/interim-memo-ml-26-002-nasas-management-of-programs-and-projects-after-mission-termination-artemis-campaign-systems.pdf)).

The breakdown spans four programs:
- Exploration Upper Stage (EUS) — the upgraded SLS upper stage that would have replaced the ICPS for Artemis IV onward.
- Universal Stage Adapter (USA) — connecting hardware between EUS and Orion.
- Mobile Launcher 2 (ML-2) — the second mobile launcher, redesigned for the taller EUS configuration.
- Gateway HALO module — the habitation and logistics outpost in lunar orbit.

OIG's audit also documented contract growth: across the eight projects reviewed, total cost overruns averaged 110.71 percent above original baselines. Aviation Week characterized the report as identifying "significant cost-cut opportunities" if NASA continues the cancellation pattern under the FY27 budget proposal ([Aviation Week](https://aviationweek.com/space/space-exploration/nasa-ig-artemis-revisions-hold-promise-significant-cost-cuts)).

This matters for the Sunday-newsletter audience for two reasons. First, the next NASA budget cycle hinges on whether Congress accepts the administration's restructuring of Artemis. Second, the OIG report explicitly recommends NASA develop a formal "transition framework" for canceled programs — i.e., what to do with the people, contracts, and physical hardware. The agency has not yet responded publicly.

## Lunar surface assets: rover race continues

The Lunar Terrain Vehicle competition is now down to two contenders for the late-2027 deployment slot. Astrolab and Lunar Outpost are both targeting unpressurized-rover demonstrations on the Moon ahead of Artemis IV crew arrival ([Spaceflight Now](https://spaceflightnow.com/2026/06/26/next-gen-astronaut-moon-rovers-aim-for-deployment-ahead-of-artemis-4-crew-arrival/)). NASA awarded both companies feasibility-phase contracts last year and is expected to down-select to a single provider for the surface demonstration mission later in 2026.

Separately, Blue Origin confirmed last week that the first Blue Moon Mk1 lander launch has slipped from late 2026 to early 2027. The lander itself is reportedly ready; the slip is tied to LC-36 reconstruction and a New Glenn return-to-flight that Blue Origin still says it can achieve before year-end.

## What to watch

A few items that are quiet right now but could move:

- The FAA's mishap investigation closure for Starship Flight 12. SpaceX is leading the review; FAA sign-off is the formal gate to Flight 13.
- Any NASA response to the OIG memo. The agency has 30 days to provide formal comments, which would put a reply window in mid-to-late July.
- Atlas V LA-08 launch outcome on July 2. If it slips, it pushes the Atlas V end-of-life calendar.
- Congressional reaction to the OIG numbers. The House Science Committee has Artemis hearings tentatively scheduled for mid-July.

## Where this leaves us

Three weeks ago, the Artemis III storyline was "engines arriving." Two weeks ago, it was "weather cover arriving." This week, it is "booster stacking begins." That is real progress — hardware in vertical integration is the first visible step toward a launch.

At the same time, the OIG memo is a reminder that the program's funded path is not the program's planned path. Five-point-nine billion dollars in canceled hardware is the cost of restructuring under way; the question Congress will press in the autumn is whether the savings from those cancellations are large enough, and timely enough, to keep Artemis III on its current schedule without forcing further changes downstream.

Starship's slip from this week to later in July is not, by itself, a problem for Artemis. The propellant-transfer demonstrations are still months away in any case. But each slip narrows the margin between Starship qualification and the moment NASA needs the lander to be operationally available — and that margin is the thing most likely to drive the Artemis III date past mid-2027 if it disappears.
