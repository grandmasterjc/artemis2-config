# The Week Ahead: August 3–9, 2026

A short briefing on what to watch in human lunar exploration and the surrounding launch market over the next seven days.

---

## What's actually launching

A thin week, and mostly routine.

- Long March 8A: August 4, 08:50 UTC from Wenchang.
- Falcon 9 / Starlink 17-53: August 4, 14:00 UTC from Vandenberg SLC-4E.
- Smart Dragon 3: August 5, 02:35 UTC, sea launch from Haiyang.
- Falcon 9: August 5, 07:42 UTC from Cape Canaveral.
- H3-22: August 6, 19:30 UTC from Tanegashima. Japan's H3 continues its steady run toward becoming the country's workhorse.
- Falcon 9: August 8, 14:00 UTC from Vandenberg.
- Long March 7A: August 9, 11:00 UTC from Wenchang.

Nothing here is Artemis hardware. The Moon program's movement this week happens on the ground.

## Artemis 3: waiting on engines

Booster stacking inside the Vehicle Assembly Building has been under way since July 11, when the left and right aft assemblies were secured to mobile launcher 1. Segments continue to go vertical.

The item worth tracking is the core stage. NASA connected the upper four-fifths of the stage to its engine section in May, and the first two RS-25 engines reached the VAB in June. All four have to be installed before the core stage can be stacked between the boosters, which makes engine installation the gate on everything downstream.

Watch for NASA imagery or a blog update confirming the remaining engines are in place. That is the milestone that turns a stack of boosters into a rocket.

## Starship: the orbital flight starts to firm up

Flight 13 on July 24 was the program's best day so far: 20 Starlink V3 satellites deployed, a successful in-space Raptor relight, and a Ship that survived splashdown intact in the Indian Ocean. The booster's landing burn lit five engines instead of 13 and hit the water hard, which remains the open item.

Flight 14 is the one that matters for Artemis. Reporting from late July indicates SpaceX has been cleared to attempt the first Ship catch by the launch tower on that flight, paired with the first genuine orbital profile and a controlled return. Targets range from late in the third quarter to September depending on the source, and Gwynne Shotwell has said the company intends to fly monthly. Treat any specific date as soft.

The ship-to-ship propellant transfer demonstration, the test the Artemis 4 landing actually depends on, is a separate mission and currently sits no earlier than late 2026.

## Blue Origin against the clock

Blue Origin says the rebuilt LC-36A will be ready before the end of the year, and the company is still publicly targeting a New Glenn return to flight in 2026. Debris removal and hardware recovery are done, reconstruction is under way, and the tank farm, integration facility, access tower and water tower all survived the May explosion. So did a twice-flown first stage and three second stages, which is why the schedule is merely difficult rather than impossible.

Endurance, the Blue Moon MK1 lander for NASA's first Moon Base mission to the lunar south pole, finished thermal vacuum testing at Johnson earlier this year. It is ready. It is also waiting on a rocket.

## Five things that could move this week

Confirmation that the remaining RS-25 engines are installed on the Artemis 3 core stage. A firm date for Starship Flight 14. Any NASA statement on the Flight 13 booster landing anomaly. Construction milestones from LC-36. And the FAA licence modification Starship would need for a tower catch of the upper stage, which has to be public before that flight can happen.

Quiet weeks are when schedules either quietly recover or quietly slip. This is one of those.
