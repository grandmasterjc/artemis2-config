# The Week Ahead: September 28–October 4, 2026

A short briefing on what to watch in human lunar exploration and the surrounding launch market over the next seven days.

---

## What's actually launching

The busiest week this newsletter has covered in a month, front-loaded with Starship and closing with a rare double launch from Florida.

- Starship Flight 14: targeted for Monday, September 28, at 12:15 UTC, from Starbase's Pad 2, with a 75-minute window and backup opportunities September 29 and 30. Ship 41 and Booster 21 completed a full launch rehearsal on September 24 and are fully stacked; as of this writing SpaceX still needs the FAA to issue the actual flight license, not just confirm the target date ([Space.com](https://www.space.com/space-exploration/launches-spacecraft/spacex-starship-flight-14-1st-orbital-launch-what-time)).
- Crew-13: targeted for Thursday, October 1, no earlier than 15:10 UTC (11:10 a.m. EDT), Falcon 9 from Cape Canaveral SLC-40, with docking at the ISS's Harmony module roughly nine hours later ([NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/blogs/spacestation/2026/09/22/nasa-spacex-move-to-final-crew-13-prelaunch-prep-for-oct-1-liftoff/)).
- NROL-97: targeted for the same evening, liftoff at 23:53 EDT October 1 (03:53 UTC October 2), Falcon Heavy from Kennedy Space Center's LC-39A, with a backup 24 hours later. It is the first National Reconnaissance Office payload to fly on Falcon Heavy after 22 missions on Falcon 9 ([SpaceX](https://www.spacex.com/launches/nrol97)).

Two Falcon boosters are due to land at Cape Canaveral's LZ-1 and LZ-2 the same night NASA sends a crew to orbit from the pad next door, which is the kind of range-sharing that does not happen often.

## Starship attempts its first full orbit

Every previous Starship flight has been suborbital by design, deliberately shy of a full lap so a malfunctioning ship comes down over ocean rather than over land. Flight 14 drops that safety margin. If it flies, Ship 41 is meant to reach a sustained 275-kilometer orbit, circle Earth roughly six times over nearly ten hours, and deploy 26 of the larger Starlink V3 satellites, the first time that hardware has reached orbit at all. Booster 21 comes down offshore in the Gulf rather than at the tower; Ship 41 targets a Pacific splashdown well west of Chile rather than a catch, which SpaceX has said will wait for a later flight. The mission has already slipped past this newsletter twice, from September 15 to the 18th to the 28th, each time for reasons SpaceX did not fully explain. The hardware is now the least uncertain part of the equation; the FAA's paperwork is the last thing standing between a fully stacked, rehearsed vehicle and a launch attempt.

## Isaacman promises an "Artemis acceleration" update as Crew-13 flies

NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman told an industry audience at Houston's Off World Summit on September 25 that the agency will lay out an "Artemis acceleration" update in early October, covering the Moon base buildout, lander cadence at the south pole, and nuclear power and propulsion work, and said 2027 is meant to be "the year of execution" without further pivots. Payload Space reports the update may land around Crew-13's own launch, which would put a substantial policy announcement inside a week already carrying two Cape Canaveral launches. Nothing has been published yet, so treat both the date and the specifics as provisional until NASA actually puts something out.

## Artemis 3's boosters near their last segment while the core stage waits

Inside the Vehicle Assembly Building, the twin five-segment boosters for Artemis 3's SLS rocket are down to their forward assemblies, the last piece needed to complete each stack, after crews finished mating the forward-center segments in the past two weeks. Core Stage-3, sitting nearby with all four RS-25 engines now installed, still has no announced date for moving onto Mobile Launcher 1 between the two boosters. That transfer, not the booster work, is the step that turns two large finished assemblies into one stacked rocket, and NASA has not said this week when it will happen.

## Five things that could move this week

Whether the FAA actually issues Flight 14's license in time for September 28, or the backup days on the 29th and 30th become the real target. Whether NASA and SpaceX confirm both halves of Thursday's planned double launch, Crew-13 and NROL-97, on schedule rather than staggering them further apart. What Isaacman's promised Artemis acceleration update actually contains, and whether it arrives inside this window at all. Whether NASA names a transfer date for moving Artemis 3's core stage onto Mobile Launcher 1 now that the boosters are nearly done. And whether Flight 14's outcome changes the October 19 target already pencilled in for Flight 15, the mission that would make the first real attempt at catching a returning ship.
