# The Week Ahead: September 14–20, 2026

A short briefing on what to watch in human lunar exploration and the surrounding launch market over the next seven days.

---

## What's actually launching

A lighter week than most, and not one led by a crew launch.

- Soyuz 2.1b: targeted for September 16, around 13:33 UTC, from Baikonur. Payload undisclosed as of this writing.
- A Chinese launch from Wenchang, around 00:30 UTC on September 17, followed the same day by another from Jiuquan, around 02:35 UTC. Neither payload has been confirmed; Chinese domestic missions routinely go unannounced until after liftoff, so treat both times as soft ([Space Launch Schedule](https://www.spacelaunchschedule.com/)).
- Starship Flight 14: Friday, September 18, targeting 12:15 UTC from Starbase's Pad 2, with a window that runs to 14:14 UTC.
- A third Chinese launch, from Taiyuan, around 10:50 UTC on September 19. Payload undisclosed.
- Falcon 9 / Starlink: September 20, around 01:47 UTC, from Vandenberg SLC-4E, carrying 27 satellites.

None of that is Artemis hardware. That work is happening on the ground at Kennedy and at Starbase, and on a calendar three weeks out at the Cape.

## Crew-13 gets a firm date again, three weeks out

Crew-13 came off the pad on August 29 after teams found an oxidizer leak in Dragon's propulsion system, and for two weeks NASA had nothing more specific than "additional tests." That changed this week: NASA and SpaceX are now targeting no earlier than September 30 for the Falcon 9 launch carrying NASA's Jessica Watkins and Luke Delaney, CSA's Joshua Kutryk and Roscosmos's Sergey Teteryatnikov to the International Space Station, confirmed independently by the Canadian Space Agency and NASA's own mission page ([CSA](https://x.com/csa_asc/status/2095266508062519472), [NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/mission/nasas-spacex-crew-13/)). That date falls just outside this newsletter's window, but it is the first specific number since the leak, and worth watching for whether it holds through the next two weeks.

## Artemis 3's engines are in, and its boosters are down to their last segments

NASA finished installing all four RS-25 engines into the Artemis 3 core stage, work that started August 24 with the stage standing vertical in High Bay 2, the first time an SLS core stage has been built that way. The engines, tail numbers E2054, E2057, E2048 and E2052, are shuttle veterans; E2048 previously flew Discovery on STS-95 in 1998. Finishing the install opens the outfitting work that has to happen before the stage can move onto Mobile Launcher 1, between the two boosters ([NASASpaceFlight](https://www.nasaspaceflight.com/2026/09/artemis-iii-sls-stacking-artemis-ii-orion-fix/)). The boosters have moved too: after mating their center segments, technicians have started stacking the forward-center segments on top, leaving only the forward assemblies to finish each stack. Watch for NASA to name a date for the core-stage transfer, the clearest sign yet of how close Artemis 3 actually is to a fully stacked vehicle.

## Starship's tower catch drops off Flight 14, and the launch itself slips three days

Flight 14 has moved again. SpaceX's own scheduling had pointed to September 15, but the FCC filing behind the mission and both major flight trackers now list Friday, September 18, at 12:15 UTC. The bigger change is what the flight will not attempt. Elon Musk said on SpaceX's earnings call that Flight 14 would catch a returning Ship with the launch tower's arms, then reversed that on August 20, writing the catch would happen "probably" in "a few months" instead. That reversal has held: Ship 41 is now expected to fly a full orbital-class profile and splash down in the Indian Ocean, while the Super Heavy booster comes down in the Gulf of Mexico rather than back at the tower ([Teslarati](https://www.teslarati.com/spacex-catch-starship-launch-14-elon-musk/)). The flight still matters on its own terms. It would be Starship's first genuine orbital mission, deploying roughly 20 of the larger Starlink V3 satellites, the first time that hardware has reached orbit at all.

## Blue Origin moves its next big engine test off Florida

New Glenn's return to flight has mostly been a story about rebuilding Launch Complex 36 after May's hotfire explosion. This week added a second track: Blue Origin said on September 9 that a New Glenn second-stage unit is headed to NASA's Stennis Space Center for hot-fire testing on the B-2 stand this fall, explicitly framed around supporting Artemis missions in 2027 and beyond rather than testing everything at the damaged Cape site ([Blue Origin](https://www.blueorigin.com/news/blue-origin-nasa-partner-to-test-new-glenn-at-stennis-space-center)). Back at the Cape, the company has also broken ground on an $80 million, 120,000-square-foot payload processing building meant to support up to 16 additional missions a year once it opens in early 2028, and LC-36B's foundation work is now visible as sheet piling goes into the ground ([Hoodline](https://hoodline.com/2026/09/blue-origin-breaks-ground-on-80m-cape-canaveral-payload-facility-for-16-missions-a-year/)). None of this changes Blue Origin's own target of a New Glenn return to flight before the end of 2026; it is infrastructure for the flights after that one.

## Five things that could move this week

Whether Starship actually launches on September 18 without another slip, and whether SpaceX confirms in writing that no tower catch will be attempted. Whether NASA sets a date for moving Artemis 3's core stage onto Mobile Launcher 1. Whether the forward booster segments go on cleanly enough to finish stacking this week. Whether Blue Origin's second-stage unit actually ships to Stennis, rather than just being announced. And whether the September 30 Crew-13 date survives the next two weeks without slipping again.
