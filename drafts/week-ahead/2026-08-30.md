# The Week Ahead: August 31–September 6, 2026

A short briefing on what to watch in human lunar exploration and the surrounding launch market over the next seven days.

---

## What's actually launching

A quiet week on the manifest, and a short one.

- Electron / StriX Launch 11 ("Owl Around The World"): August 31, 12:05 UTC, from Rocket Lab Launch Complex 1, Mahia Peninsula, New Zealand. An 11th dedicated Rocket Lab mission for Japan's Synspective, carrying a single StriX synthetic-aperture-radar satellite to a 575 km orbit ([SpaceWatch.Global](https://spacewatch.global/2026/08/synspective-sets-31-august-launch-for-11th-strix-constellation-satellite-on-rocket-lab-electron/)).
- Falcon 9 / Starlink 15-23: September 2, 07:46 UTC, from Vandenberg SLC-4E.
- Falcon 9 / Starlink 15-24: September 6, 10:59 UTC, from Vandenberg SLC-4E.

None of that is Artemis hardware. That work is happening on the ground, at Kennedy and at Starbase.

## NASA starts bolting engines into the Artemis 3 core stage

The four RS-25 engines that reached the Vehicle Assembly Building on July 21 sat mated to the engine section for weeks while technicians worked through the summer. On August 24, that changed: NASA began actually installing the engines into the SLS core stage, still standing vertical in High Bay 2, the first time an SLS core stage has been built this way ([NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/blogs/missions/2026/08/27/nasa-starts-artemis-iii-engine-install-boosters-crew-training-advance/)). Each engine produces nearly 500,000 pounds of thrust and will burn for more than eight minutes at liftoff; one of the four, engine E2048, previously flew Space Shuttle Discovery on STS-95 in 1998.

The rest of the vehicle moved too. Solid rocket booster segments are now more than halfway stacked on the mobile launcher. Orion's joined crew and service modules got their electrical, data and fluid connections completed, and NASA powered on the full spacecraft for the first time to confirm every subsystem could talk to the others. The four-person crew, NASA's Frank Rubio, Andre Douglas and Randy Bresnik plus ESA's Luca Parmitano, has moved into water-survival drills, spacesuit work in the Neutral Buoyancy Lab and integrated simulations as a full crew. NASA has still not put a date on when all four engines will be fully seated; watch for that confirmation as the week's clearest signal of how integration is actually tracking.

## Starship's tower catch waits months longer, and the launch itself slips

On SpaceX's August 4 earnings call, Elon Musk said the company would attempt to catch a returning Ship with the launch tower's arms on the very next flight, "tentatively scheduled for the end of this month." That plan did not survive August. On August 20, Musk reversed course on social media, writing that SpaceX would "probably catch the ship with the tower in a few months" instead, and internal SpaceX planning documents now point to a September 15 target for Flight 14 itself, pushed back from the earlier late-August date ([SpaceNews](https://spacenews.com/spacex-scales-back-plans-for-next-starship-launch/)). NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman had floated an early-September date as recently as August 14, so even that window is already looking optimistic. Flight 14 is still billed as Starship's first genuine orbital mission, deploying Starlink V3 satellites to an operational orbit, a step up from the suborbital-plus-splashdown profile Flight 13 flew on July 24. None of that changes the separate, still-undated ship-to-ship propellant transfer demonstration that Artemis 4's lander actually depends on.

## Blue Origin's retrofit valve hardware comes due this week

Blue Origin traced May's New Glenn pad explosion to the main oxygen valve on one of the booster's seven BE-4 engines. CEO Dave Limp said the fix is a small, quickly-retrofittable modification to the valve rather than a redesign, and that the updated hardware would be ready "by the end of this month," meaning by the close of the week this newsletter covers ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/science/2026/08/06/blue-origin-blames-new-glenn-fireball-on-engine-oxygen-valve/5283997)). Limp has not attached a specific return-to-flight date beyond Blue Origin's standing goal of launching again before the end of 2026. Separately, the company is also planning a second Cape Canaveral pad, LC-36B, built for the larger New Glenn 9x4 and including its own vertical integration and payload processing facilities, though leadership has been clear that rebuilding the existing LC-36A and flying again this year stays the priority for now ([SpaceNews](https://spacenews.com/blue-origin-outlines-plans-for-second-cape-canaveral-launch-pad/)).

## Five things that could move this week

Whether NASA confirms all four RS-25 engines are fully seated in the Artemis 3 core stage. Whether Blue Origin's retrofit valve hardware actually clears manufacturing by its end-of-month target, or slips into September. Any firmer word from SpaceX on the September 15 Flight 14 target, given how quickly the August 28 date came and went. Progress on Artemis 3's booster stacking, now past the halfway mark on the mobile launcher. And whether Orion's newly powered-on systems clear their checks cleanly enough for NASA to say so publicly.
