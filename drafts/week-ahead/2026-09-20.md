# The Week Ahead: September 21–27, 2026

A short briefing on what to watch in human lunar exploration and the surrounding launch market over the next seven days.

---

## What's actually launching

A quiet week again, and once more without a crewed mission or Artemis hardware on the pad.

- Long March 8A: targeted for September 23, around 13:30 UTC, from Wenchang. Payload undisclosed as of this writing; Chinese domestic missions routinely go unannounced until after liftoff, so treat the date as soft ([Space Launch Schedule](https://www.spacelaunchschedule.com/)).
- Long March 6A: targeted for September 24, around 08:45 UTC, from Taiyuan. Payload also undisclosed.
- Electron / StriX Launch 13 ("Owlright, Owlright, Owlright"): September 26, around 00:15 UTC, from Rocket Lab Launch Complex 1, Mahia Peninsula. A 13th dedicated Rocket Lab mission for Japan's Synspective, carrying another StriX synthetic-aperture-radar satellite.
- Falcon 9 / USSF-385: September 26, window opens 11:56 UTC, from Vandenberg SLC-4E, with a backup opportunity September 27. A classified US Space Force payload ([SpaceX](https://www.spacex.com/launches/ussf385)).

Neither Starship nor a crew launch makes the list. Both slipped out of this window this week, for different reasons.

## Starship's Flight 14 slips past this newsletter for a third time

Last week's edition of this newsletter had Flight 14 targeting September 18. That did not hold. The FAA's own operations plan moved the window to September 22, and then, in a September 17 post on X, SpaceX pushed it again to Monday, September 28, "pending regulatory approval," without giving a reason for either slip ([TeslaNorth](https://teslanorth.com/2026/09/17/spacex-starship-flight-14-september-28/)). The hardware has kept busy through the delay: SpaceX used Starbase's Pad 2 on September 12 to rehearse ship-catch operations with Ship 42, the next flight's hardware, suggesting some of the wait is about infrastructure readiness rather than Ship 41 or Booster 21 themselves. The mission profile is unchanged: Starship would reach orbit for the first time, circle Earth roughly six times, and deploy 26 of the larger Starlink V3 satellites, with the booster coming down offshore and the ship making a controlled Pacific Ocean splashdown rather than a tower catch. September 28 falls just outside this window, but a real FAA license, not just a target date, is what to watch for before then.

## Artemis 3's twin boosters are essentially done; the core stage still isn't on the launcher

NASA said on September 15 that the twin solid rocket boosters for Artemis 3's SLS rocket are all but complete, each one 177 feet tall and together good for more than 75 percent of the 8.8 million pounds of thrust the rocket needs to leave the pad. That milestone landed alongside a stalled one: all four RS-25 engines are now installed on Core Stage-3, finishing the work this newsletter tracked in August and early September, but NASA has still not set a date for moving the stage onto Mobile Launcher 1 between the two finished boosters. Until that transfer happens, the boosters and the core stage stay two separate, mostly complete pieces of the same rocket rather than one stacked vehicle, and a transfer date is the clearest next signal of how Artemis 3 is tracking toward 2027.

## Crew-13 slips into October as its crew starts quarantine

The September 30 target this newsletter reported last week did not hold either. NASA and SpaceX are now targeting early October for Crew-13, a further slip from the oxidizer leak found in Dragon's propulsion system in late August. The four-person crew, NASA's Jessica Watkins and Luke Delaney, CSA's Joshua Kutryk and Roscosmos's Sergey Teteryatnikov, entered standard prelaunch quarantine at Johnson Space Center late on September 17, limiting in-person contact ahead of the flight ([NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/blogs/commercialcrew/2026/09/17/nasa-spacex-continue-crew-13-prep-as-crew-enters-quarantine/)). Quarantine starting is normally a sign a launch date is close to firm; NASA has not yet said which specific October date that means.

## Blue Origin lines up its Stennis test while rebuilding at the Cape

Under a July agreement with NASA, Blue Origin plans to start hot-fire testing a New Glenn second-stage unit on the B-2 stand at NASA's Stennis Space Center "this fall," explicitly framed around Artemis missions from 2027 onward. No specific test date has been set yet. At Cape Canaveral, the company is not rebuilding Launch Complex 36 as it stood before May's hotfire explosion; instead it is moving to a hybrid horizontal and vertical launch setup it had already been developing for the larger New Glenn 9x4, reusing surviving infrastructure such as the propellant farm and the damaged support tower rather than replacing it outright. Blue Origin's standing target remains a New Glenn return to flight before the end of 2026.

## Five things that could move this week

Whether SpaceX's September 28 target for Flight 14 holds, or the FAA license itself becomes the next thing to slip. Whether NASA names a transfer date for moving Artemis 3's core stage onto Mobile Launcher 1. Whether NASA and SpaceX narrow "early October" to a firm Crew-13 date now that the crew is in quarantine. Whether either Chinese Long March payload gets identified after liftoff. And whether Blue Origin's Stennis test unit actually ships this week, rather than staying an autumn promise.
