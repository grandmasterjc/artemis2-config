# The Week Ahead — June 22–28, 2026

A short briefing on what to watch in human lunar exploration and the surrounding launch market over the next seven days.

---

## What's actually launching

It's a thin manifest. Most of the action this week is on test pads and inside assembly buildings, not on the way to orbit.

- Pegasus-XL with Katalyst's LINK on-orbit servicing spacecraft — targeted Saturday, June 27 from Kwajalein Atoll, Marshall Islands. The mission will attempt to attach to NASA's Neil Gehrels Swift Observatory and boost it back to a stable orbit ([SpacePolicyOnline](https://spacepolicyonline.com/news/whats-happening-in-space-policy-june-21-27-2026/)).
- Starlink Group 17-45 — June 28, 14:00 UTC from Vandenberg, Falcon 9 ([Next2Space](https://next2space.com/schedule/)).
- A handful of other Falcon 9 Starlink batches are expected to slot in. SpaceX rarely confirms the full week's manifest more than 48 hours out.

The interesting thing here isn't what's flying — it's what isn't. There is no New Glenn this week (LC-36 is still being rebuilt after the May 28 static-fire explosion), no Starship (Flight 13 is targeting July 3 at earliest), and no SLS (Artemis II is past its launch and Artemis III is months from rollout).

## Artemis III: engines move into the VAB

The most consequential progress in the Artemis program this week is happening quietly inside Kennedy's Vehicle Assembly Building.

NASA published images on June 16 showing the first of four RS-25 engines being unboxed in the VAB after transport from Stennis Space Center in Mississippi. The engine, serial number 2052, is the first to arrive at KSC for Artemis III core stage installation ([Space Scout](https://www.spacescout.info/2026/06/nasa-prepares-next-artemis-rocket-for-flight/)).

What to watch this week:
- Whether the remaining three RS-25 engines arrive at KSC. They are reportedly being staged at Stennis pending EGS readiness to receive them.
- Any update on the SLS "spacer" segment — the one-off replacement for the upper stage that, for Artemis III, will sit where an ICPS would normally go. Artemis program manager Jeremy Parsons said earlier this month that fabrication has begun, but no completion date has been published.
- All eight booster segments are already at KSC. The unique spacer is the last major hardware item still in production ([Space Scout](https://www.spacescout.info/2026/06/nasa-prepares-next-artemis-rocket-for-flight/)).

NASA has said booster stacking begins "this summer" with a tanking test at Pad 39B by end of year. That timeline depends on the engine section being mated to the rest of the core stage on schedule — and that, in turn, depends on no further surprises during engine installation.

## Starship Flight 13: static fires due

SpaceX is targeting the final week of June for static-fire tests of both Booster 20 and Ship 40 at Starbase, in preparation for Flight 13 ([MEXC](https://www.mexc.com/learn/article/spacex-starship-2026-latest-launches-flight-test-timeline-and-what-comes-next/1)).

What that means concretely:
- Booster 20: full-duration static fire of its 33 Raptor 3 engines. Cryogenic proof testing is already complete.
- Ship 40: separate sea-level and vacuum Raptor verification. New heat-shield tile configuration was structurally verified earlier this month.
- Target launch date: July 3, 2026, pending FAA review.

The relevance for Artemis: Flight 13 is the next step in qualifying the Starship vehicle that will eventually fly as the HLS lander for Artemis III. SpaceX needs to demonstrate full and rapid reusability before the propellant-transfer demonstrations that Artemis III docking depends on can begin in earnest. Flight 12 deployed 22 Starlink simulators successfully but lost the booster on landing. A clean Flight 13 — both halves — would be the first.

## Blue Origin: still rebuilding

Blue Origin began formal reconstruction of Launch Complex 36 on June 16, eighteen days after the New Glenn static-fire explosion that destroyed both the rocket and the pad.

At VivaTech in Paris on June 17, founder Jeff Bezos and CEO Dave Limp said the company intends to return New Glenn to flight before the end of 2026 ([India Today](https://www.indiatoday.in/amp/science/story/blue-origin-new-glenn-rocket-explosion-rebuild-launch-pad-2026-science-news-2929320-2026-06-18)). That is significantly more optimistic than the initial NASA assessment, which suggested the pad would not be operational until 2028.

Limp said the company will move to an "alternative vertical pre-launch scheme" for getting rockets onto the pad, removing the need to rebuild the destroyed transporter-erector ([Look Into The Space](https://lookintothe.space/kosmichni-novyny/blue-origin-rozpochala-vidnovlennia-maidanchyka-lc-36-i-planuie-zapusk-new-glenn-do-kintsia-2026-roku)).

This week's tell: any visible movement at LC-36 (debris is reportedly fully cleared) and whether Blue Origin publishes a more specific timeline for the next New Glenn vehicle's status. The Blue Moon 1 lunar lander remains stranded — both the rocket and the pad it was supposed to launch from are still recovering.

## What's quiet that might not stay quiet

A few things to keep half an eye on:

- The FAA's response to the SpaceX Flight 12 booster mishap investigation. SpaceX is leading the investigation; FAA approval of corrective actions is the gate to Flight 13.
- The NASA Aerospace Safety Advisory Panel meets virtually on Monday afternoon. These are not usually news-makers, but Artemis III risk posture sometimes surfaces in the minutes.
- Pre-launch interviews with NASA astronaut Anil Menon from Star City on Monday morning — routine, but worth noting as the next Soyuz crew rotation builds momentum.

## Where this leaves us

The story of June 2026 in lunar exploration is a story of patience. Artemis II flew successfully in April. Artemis III hardware is converging at Kennedy. Starship is two flights into its V3 architecture and still working out booster recovery. Blue Origin is rebuilding from a setback that, six weeks ago, looked like it might cost them the Artemis V landing slot — and they're now publicly betting they can fly again this year.

None of these stories will resolve this week. But the next seven days will tell us whether the supporting work — engines, static fires, pad reconstruction — is keeping pace with the published schedules. If it is, autumn becomes interesting.
