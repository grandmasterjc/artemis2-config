# The Week Ahead: August 24–30, 2026

A short briefing on what to watch in human lunar exploration and the surrounding launch market over the next seven days.

---

## What's actually launching

The busiest week on the manifest in a while, headlined by a mission that is not American.

- Chang'e 7 / Long March 5: China's most ambitious lunar mission yet, carrying an orbiter, lander, rover and a hopping probe, targets liftoff from Wenchang around August 24–25, with sources splitting between 00:05 and 00:30 UTC on the 25th, so treat the exact hour as soft ([SpaceLaunchSchedule](https://www.spacelaunchschedule.com/), [Space.com](https://www.space.com/astronomy/moon/chinas-change-7-moon-probe-will-launch-this-weekend-on-the-most-ambitious-lunar-mission-in-history)).
- Long March 6C: August 25, roughly 03:20 UTC, from Taiyuan, payload undisclosed as of this writing.
- Falcon 9 / Starlink 10-49: August 25, 05:49 UTC, from Cape Canaveral SLC-40.
- Falcon 9 / Starlink 15-22: August 25, 09:42 UTC, from Vandenberg SLC-4E.
- Ariane 62 / MTG-I2: August 27, 20:10 UTC, from Kourou, a European weather satellite.
- Falcon Heavy / Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope: August 30, 11:26 UTC, from Kennedy Space Center, NASA's next flagship observatory.

None of that is Artemis hardware, but Chang'e 7 is the closest thing to direct lunar competition this newsletter has covered.

## Chang'e 7 heads for the same crater rim NASA wants

Chang'e 7 is aimed at the illuminated rim of Shackleton crater, near the lunar south pole, the same permanently-shadowed neighborhood where NASA's Artemis and CLPS landers are hunting for water ice. The mission's hopping probe carries a water-molecule and hydrogen-isotope analyzer built specifically to reach into shadowed crater floors a rover cannot enter ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chang%27e_7)). It is a precursor for China's own crewed lunar ambitions and a reminder that the ice-hunting phase of the new Moon race is not a solo American effort. Watch for confirmation of a clean translunar injection in the days after launch.

## Artemis 3: engines still going in, no completion date yet

All four RS-25 engines reached the Vehicle Assembly Building on July 21 and technicians have spent the summer bolting them to the core stage with it standing vertical in High Bay 2, a first for SLS. NASA has still not said the installation is finished ([NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/blogs/missions/2026/08/05/nasa-joins-artemis-iii-orion-modules-rocket-hardware-update/)). Orion's crew and service modules, joined on July 30, are now in the phase where technicians complete the electrical, data and fluid connections between them before power-up and integrated testing. Nothing new moved on either front this week; both remain the milestones worth watching for.

## Starship: Flight 14's date is getting less certain, not more

Flight 13 on July 24 was the program's cleanest outing yet, deploying 20 Starlink V3 satellites and surviving an Indian Ocean splashdown intact, though the booster's landing burn lit five engines instead of thirteen and hit the water hard. For Flight 14, SpaceX wants a genuine orbital profile and the first attempt to catch a returning Ship with the launch tower's arms. An FCC filing lists August 28 as a target, but other reporting this month points to a no-earlier-than September date, and FAA clearance for the expanded landing-zone footprint a catch requires is still pending ([BigGo Finance](https://finance.biggo.com/news/4e87090d-2c58-4718-82e3-c346b1173000)). Treat late August as the optimistic case, not the likely one.

## Blue Origin: the valve fix comes due, and the Moon lander slips a year

The retrofit hardware for the oxygen valve blamed for May's New Glenn explosion is due out of manufacturing by the end of this week, with testing continuing at Stennis Space Center into the fall ([Spaceflight Now](https://spaceflightnow.com/2026/08/06/blue-origin-identifies-engine-issue-behind-new-glenn-explosion/)). Separately, and with less fanfare, Blue Origin's lunar chief said Endurance, the Blue Moon Mk1 lander for NASA's first Moon Base mission, has slipped from a target of "around the end of this summer" to the first quarter of 2027. The hardware itself finished environmental testing at Johnson Space Center and is effectively done; it is being kept ready in storage until New Glenn is cleared to fly again. That is a full readiness reversal from where this newsletter left it two weeks ago, when Endurance looked like the piece waiting on the rocket rather than the other way around on any near-term calendar. Construction on the second pad, LC-36B, is also underway, with steel erection on its new Vertical Integration Facility already well along ([Blue Origin](https://www.blueorigin.com/news/returning-launch-complex-36-to-two-pads)).

## Five things that could move this week

Whether Chang'e 7 reaches translunar injection cleanly on schedule. Any NASA confirmation that all four RS-25 engines are seated in the Artemis 3 core stage. Whether SpaceX or the FAA say anything concrete about a Flight 14 date, given the August 28 filing is now in tension with September reporting. Whether Blue Origin's retrofit valve hardware actually clears manufacturing by its end-of-month target. And whether the Roman Space Telescope launch on August 30 goes off without a scrub, closing out the week with NASA's next big observatory in space.
