# The Week Ahead: November 2 to 8, 2026

A short briefing on what to watch in human lunar exploration and the surrounding launch market over the next seven days.

---

## SLS has been quiet since the Artemis III briefing

NASA's last two Artemis hardware posts are from October. The [October 13 update](https://www.nasa.gov/blogs/missions/) reported that teams at Kennedy Space Center had finished stacking the twin solid rocket boosters on the mobile launcher in a "short stack" configuration for a tanking test. The [October 23 post](https://www.nasa.gov/blogs/missions/2026/10/23/nasa-shares-artemis-acceleration-plans-for-moon-missions/) said Artemis III will send four astronauts to low Earth orbit in 2027 and that the 2028 landing goal is progressing, without new hardware detail.

I found no NASA announcement since then. The next visible step would be lifting the core stage, with its four RS-25 engines already installed, onto the stacked boosters in the Vehicle Assembly Building. Reporting puts the tanking test at Pad 39B around the end of 2026, but NASA has given no date. Lockheed Martin has said it expects to deliver the Artemis III Orion to NASA at the end of this calendar year, which is also not a firm date.

## Vulcan's first flight since the booster nozzle failure moves to mid-November

Last week's edition listed Vulcan's Amazon Leo LV-01 mission for Thursday, October 29. It did not fly. [Spaceflight Now](https://spaceflightnow.com/tag/vulcan-leo-01/) now says only "mid-November" for the return, which will carry 40 Amazon Leo satellites and is the first Vulcan launch since a solid rocket booster nozzle failure on an earlier flight. [Space Right Now](https://spacerightnow.com/launches) lists November 13 at the earliest, with no time and a status of "to be determined." Nothing in the next seven days should be read as a Vulcan launch date.

## Launches, in UTC

Times are from [Space Right Now](https://spacerightnow.com/launches), cross-checked against [Orbital Radar's November 1 briefing](https://orbitalradar.com/blog/orbital-briefing-2026-11-01) and the [Wikipedia launch list](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_spaceflight_launches_in_October%E2%80%93December_2026). Where they disagree, the disagreement is stated.

- Electron / The Sea God Surveys (iQPS): Monday, November 2, 16:41 UTC, from Mahia, New Zealand, carrying one QPS-SAR radar imaging satellite. Orbital Radar marks it "Go for Launch." One other aggregator gave November 4, so the date has moved once.
- Kinetica 2 / SpaceSail: Tuesday, November 3, around 00:10 UTC, from Jiuquan. The payload name is provisional.
- Falcon 9 / USSF-432: Tuesday, November 3, 01:42 UTC, from Vandenberg SLC-4E. The listing gives the mission name only.
- Ceres-2 and a South Korean Mir test flight: both listed for Wednesday, November 4, around 21:00 and 05:00 UTC respectively, but flagged "to be confirmed" in one source and absent from the other two. I would not plan around either.
- Long March 2C: Thursday, November 5, around 16:00 UTC, from Xichang, payload unannounced.
- Long March 12: Thursday, November 5, around 21:15 UTC, from Wenchang, listed as a SatNet LEO group with the name marked uncertain.
- Falcon 9 / Starlink Group 15-21: Friday, November 6, 02:21 UTC, from Vandenberg. This mission has slipped about four days already, and one tracker now says November 10 at the earliest.

Chinese launch times are approximate and often change without notice. Launches in the last days of the week may not appear on trackers until midweek.

## Starship Flight 15 has a month, not a date

SpaceX has not published a launch date. [Space Launch Schedule](https://www.spacelaunchschedule.com/launch/starship-flight-15/) updated its entry on October 28 to "NET November," while its FAQ shows a placeholder of November 30. One secondary report cites an FAA planning marker of November 8 as an optimistic baseline, and Elon Musk's October 10 estimate of four to six weeks points to mid or late November. None of that comes from SpaceX or the FAA directly. The mission is expected to attempt the first ship catch from Starbase's second tower. A static fire of the Flight 15 booster or ship, which I did not find reported, would be the first real schedule signal.

## New Glenn is reported at early 2027, against Blue Origin's year-end target

Blue Origin has said New Glenn will return to flight before the end of 2026 after the May 28 static-fire explosion at Pad 36. Reuters reporting from October 16, relayed by secondary outlets, says early 2027 is now expected. Blue Origin has not announced a revised window. I could not verify any report of second-stage testing at Stennis Space Center this week. This matters for Artemis because the Artemis III plan, as reported, depends on a Blue Moon Mark 2 test article reaching orbit on New Glenn before Orion launches.

## Budget and oversight news is thin

NASA's FY2027 request proposed a 23 percent cut, and both House and Senate hearings in April drew bipartisan pushback. I could not find whether Congress has passed full-year appropriations or a continuing resolution since the October 1 deadline, and I found no new NASA inspector general report on Artemis. If one of those lands this week, it will be covered here next Sunday.

## Which of these dates are firm

The Electron, Falcon 9 and Long March 2C entries are marked go by at least one tracker and are the likeliest to fly on time. The Ceres-2, Mir and Long March 12 entries are the softest. The Vulcan, Starship and New Glenn dates are all unofficial.
