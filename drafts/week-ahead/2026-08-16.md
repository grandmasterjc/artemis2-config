# The Week Ahead: August 17–23, 2026

A short briefing on what to watch in human lunar exploration and the surrounding launch market over the next seven days.

---

## What's actually launching

A quiet week on the manifest, dominated by Starlink.

- Falcon 9 / Starlink 17-50: window opens August 19, 02:00 UTC, from Vandenberg SLC-4E.
- Falcon 9 / Starlink 10-39: August 20, 15:19 UTC, from Cape Canaveral SLC-40.
- Falcon 9 / Starlink 15-20: August 22, 07:25 UTC, from Vandenberg SLC-4E.
- Long March 2C: targeted for August 17 from Taiyuan, payload undisclosed as of this writing. Chinese domestic missions routinely go unannounced until after liftoff, so treat the date as soft ([Space Launch Schedule](https://www.spacelaunchschedule.com/)).

None of that is Artemis hardware. That work is happening on the ground, at Kennedy, at Starbase and at Cape Canaveral.

## Artemis 3: engines start going into the core stage

All four RS-25 engines, flight-proven veterans of the shuttle program built by L3Harris, reached the Vehicle Assembly Building on July 21 after shipping from Stennis Space Center ([NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/blogs/missions/2026/08/05/nasa-joins-artemis-iii-orion-modules-rocket-hardware-update/)). Integration is under way this summer, and for the first time in SLS history technicians are installing the engines with the core stage standing vertical in High Bay 2 rather than lying on its side, the same orientation the rocket holds on the pad. NASA has not put a firm date on when all four will be seated.

Orion took its own step forward on July 30, when its crew and service modules were joined inside the Neil Armstrong Operations and Checkout Building, turning two major assemblies into one spacecraft. Power-up and integrated systems testing come next. At Marshall Space Flight Center, the spacer barrel that replaces the interim cryogenic propulsion stage completed its eighth and final weld on July 23, and inspection is under way. Watch for a NASA update confirming engine-installation progress.

## Starship: Flight 14 aims at a late-August tower catch

Flight 13 on July 24 was the program's cleanest outing yet: 20 Starlink V3 satellites deployed, an in-space Raptor relight, and a Ship that survived an Indian Ocean splashdown intact. The booster's landing burn lit five engines instead of thirteen and hit the water hard, and that remains unresolved.

For Flight 14, SpaceX wants to go further: a genuine orbital profile and, for the first time, an attempt to catch the returning Ship with the launch tower's arms. Reporting points to late August, with an FCC filing listing August 28 as a target date ([BigGo Finance](https://finance.biggo.com/news/4e87090d-2c58-4718-82e3-c346b1173000)). FAA clearance for the expanded landing-zone footprint a tower catch requires is still pending, so treat any specific date as soft. The ship-to-ship propellant transfer demonstration that Artemis 4's lander actually depends on remains a separate mission, still no earlier than late 2026.

## Blue Origin: a second pad before the first one is even finished

Blue Origin said on August 14 that it is building a second launch pad at Cape Canaveral. The existing Launch Complex 36, damaged in May's static-fire explosion, becomes 36A and keeps hosting the current New Glenn 7x2. A new 36B rises just to the north for the larger New Glenn 9x4, a vehicle with 50 percent more thrust aimed at bigger satellite constellations and lunar cargo ([NASASpaceFlight.com](https://www.nasaspaceflight.com/2026/08/blue-origin-launch-pad-cape-canaveral/)). The company has not said when 36B will be finished or when the 9x4 will fly.

On the more immediate problem, the retrofit for the oxygen valve blamed for the May explosion is still tracking toward hardware ready by the end of August, with testing continuing at Stennis Space Center this fall. Blue Origin still says it wants New Glenn flying again before the end of the year.

## Moon Base: landers are ready, the rockets underneath them are not

Endurance, the Blue Moon Mk1 lander for NASA's first Moon Base mission, finished its environmental test campaign, including thermal-vacuum testing at Johnson Space Center, and is moving toward loading cryogenic propellant ahead of final launch preparation ([NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/missions/moon-base/nasa-provides-updates-on-moon-base-cargo-landers-tech-demonstrations/)). It is close to ready. It is still waiting on New Glenn.

Three other CLPS landers are moving through their own late-stage work. Firefly's Blue Ghost Mission 2, stacked atop a separate Elytra propulsion module into a 22-foot vehicle, is aiming for the first American landing on the moon's far side. Intuitive Machines' IM-3 lander, Trinity, has finished thermal-vacuum testing ahead of a landing near Reiner Gamma's geomagnetic anomaly. And Voyager Technologies' Griffin-1 is in testing at JPL for a late-2026 launch carrying the largest commercial payload yet delivered to the lunar surface, an Astrolab rover among it.

## Five things that could move this week

Whether NASA confirms all four RS-25 engines are seated in the Artemis 3 core stage. Any FAA update on the landing-zone license Starship needs before Flight 14 can attempt a tower catch. Progress reports on Blue Origin's retrofit valve hardware against its end-of-August target. Whether ground actually breaks on the new 36B pad, or the announcement stays on paper for now. And whether Endurance's move to propellant loading holds to schedule or slips.
