# The Week Ahead: October 5 to 11, 2026

A short briefing on what to watch in human lunar exploration and the surrounding launch market over the next seven days.

---

## What's launching, and what is coming home

A lighter manifest after last week's double header, with no crewed launch and no Artemis hardware on a pad. Times are UTC and drawn from [Space Launch Schedule](https://www.spacelaunchschedule.com/) and the [Wikipedia launch list](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_spaceflight_launches_in_October%E2%80%93December_2026), which agree on every entry below.

- Falcon 9 / SDA Tranche 1 Transport Layer A: Monday, October 5, around 08:16 UTC, from Vandenberg SLC-4E, carrying 21 military communications satellites for the Space Development Agency.
- Nuri / NeonSat: Wednesday, October 7, around 03:23 UTC, from Naro Space Center in South Korea. A launch with five NeonSat Earth-observation satellites and several smaller payloads.
- Long March 12: Friday, October 9, around 19:25 UTC, from Wenchang. The payload is not announced. Chinese missions often fly with little notice and shift without explanation, so treat that date as soft.
- Falcon 9 / Starlink Group 15-25: Saturday, October 10, around 23:00 UTC, from Vandenberg, with 27 satellites.

Crew-12 is due to leave the station as well. NASA's mission page carries a no-earlier-than October 7 target for its departure now that Crew-13 has docked and joined Expedition 75. I could not confirm an undocking time, so watch NASA's blog for it. The next cargo flight, CRS-35, is listed for October 13, just outside this window.

## Starship reached orbit, and two engines did not make it easy

Flight 14 flew on September 28 and did what no earlier Starship had done: it stayed up. One of the ship's three Raptor Vacuum engines shut down early, the booster also lost an engine, and the vehicle still reached orbit, deployed all 26 Starlink V3 satellites and came down in the Pacific about 3 hours and 8 minutes after liftoff ([Space.com](https://www.space.com/space-exploration/launches-spacecraft/spacex-starship-megarocket-flight-14-orbital-launch-success), [Astronomy](https://www.astronomy.com/whats-launching-this-week/starship-reaches-orbit-on-14th-test-flight/)). Astronomy reports the ship broke apart shortly after touchdown, which was not part of the plan for a controlled splashdown.

For Artemis, the result matters because the Starship landing system cannot do much until the vehicle is routinely reaching orbit. The next hurdle is the one that decides the lunar schedule: moving propellant between two ships in orbit. I found no confirmed date for Flight 15, and the October 19 target mentioned in earlier coverage should be read as unconfirmed until the company says so.

## Both Artemis 3 boosters are fully stacked in High Bay 3

On September 30, engineers in the Vehicle Assembly Building finished integrating the nose cones onto the forward segments of both solid rocket boosters, which ended booster stacking for Artemis 3 ([NASA](https://www.friendsofnasa.org/2026/10/nasa-artemis-iii-rocket-nose-cone.html)). Each forward assembly holds an aerodynamic top, a forward skirt with avionics and the separation motors, and together the boosters supply more than 75 percent of liftoff thrust.

That clears the way for the step that matters next. Core Stage-3 has all four RS-25 engines installed, and it still needs to be lifted onto Mobile Launcher 1 between the two boosters. NASA has not published a date for that move. Artemis 3 itself is a 2027 mission, currently no earlier than June, in which a crew of four will practice docking Orion with commercial landers in low Earth orbit rather than landing.

## Isaacman's promised Artemis update has not appeared yet

At Payload's Off World conference in Houston on September 24, NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman said an "Artemis acceleration update" would arrive "sometime in early October" and might "even coincide with the Crew 13 launch" ([Payload](https://payloadspace.com/isaacman-promises-artemis-acceleration-update-next-month/)). Crew-13 launched on October 1, and I found no published update from NASA by Sunday. His earlier outline named a Moon base buildout, south pole landers on a monthly cadence starting in 2027, nuclear power and propulsion, and more science. Until NASA actually releases the document, none of that has dates attached, and "early October" leaves this week as the likeliest window.

## Blue Origin asks the FAA for up to 50 New Glenn launches a year

On September 29 the FAA released a draft environmental assessment of a Blue Origin proposal to raise launches from Cape Canaveral's Launch Complex 36 from 12 to 50 per year, according to SpaceNews coverage summarized by [AIAA](http://aiaa.org/2026/10/01/blue-origin-eyes-major-increase-in-new-glenn-launches/). The request lands while the company is still rebuilding after May's pad explosion, and Blue Origin has not flown New Glenn since. The assessment is a draft with a public comment process ahead, so it describes ambition, not approval. A hot-fire test of a New Glenn second-stage unit at NASA's Stennis Space Center is still described as coming "this fall," with no firm date.

## Four dates that could still shift

The Crew-12 undocking time, which sits on a target NASA has marked as no earlier than October 7. The date NASA gives for lifting Core Stage-3 onto the launcher, which would turn two stacked boosters into the start of one rocket. The release of the Artemis acceleration document, and whether it commits to numbers or only to direction. And the Long March 12 flight on October 9, whose payload and date are the least certain on the manifest.
