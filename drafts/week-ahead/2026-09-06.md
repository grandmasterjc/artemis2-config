# The Week Ahead: September 7–13, 2026

A short briefing on what to watch in human lunar exploration and the surrounding launch market over the next seven days.

---

## What's actually launching

A crewed launch just came off this week's manifest, so what is left is mostly cargo and national-security payloads.

- Progress MS-35 (96P): September 9, around 16:15 UTC, Soyuz 2.1b from Baikonur Site 31/6. An uncrewed Russian resupply run to the International Space Station.
- Long March 2D: targeted for September 9, around 09:00 UTC, from Jiuquan. Payload undisclosed as of this writing; Chinese domestic missions routinely go unannounced until after liftoff, so treat the date as soft ([Space Launch Schedule](https://www.spacelaunchschedule.com/)).
- USSF-153: September 10, 15:37 UTC, Falcon 9 from Vandenberg SLC-4E. A classified US Space Force payload; launch-watchers point to its booster drop zones matching recent Starlink Group 15 launches as a clue it may be a Starshield satellite batch, but neither SpaceX nor the Space Force has confirmed the payload.
- O3b mPower 11-13: September 13, around 18:40 UTC, Falcon 9 from Cape Canaveral SLC-40. The final three satellites in SES's O3b mPower medium-Earth-orbit broadband fleet, delivered by Boeing and now on the pad ([SatNews](https://satnews.com/2026/09/03/final-three-ses-o3b-mpower-satellites-arrive-at-cape-canaveral-ahead-of-falcon-9-launch/)).

None of that is Artemis hardware. The mission that was Artemis-adjacent, and the one this newsletter expected to lead with, is not launching at all.

## Crew-13 comes off the pad after a Dragon leak

Crew-13 was targeting September 12 to carry NASA's Jessica Watkins and Luke Delaney, CSA astronaut Joshua Kutryk and Roscosmos cosmonaut Sergey Teteryatnikov to the International Space Station for a 180-day increment. That date is gone. NASA and SpaceX pulled it during standard prelaunch processing after finding an oxidizer leak in the Dragon capsule's propulsion system, and as of this writing neither agency has set a new target, saying only that teams are running additional tests, reviewing data and completing whatever rework the leak requires before rescheduling ([NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/blogs/spacestation/2026/08/29/nasa-spacex-adjust-crew-13-launch-date/)). The crew assignment itself has not changed. Watch for NASA to name a new date once the propulsion system clears its checks, and for whether the fix pushes the mission past September entirely.

## Starship clears its last big test before a tower-catch attempt

Flight 14 is the mission SpaceX has spent the summer walking back and forth on: an early-September date it later called optimistic, then a same-flight tower-catch attempt Elon Musk said would slip "a few months," before an FCC filing on August 31 put a firm number back on the calendar, September 15, as the deployment date for a batch of high-capacity Starlink V3 satellites. The hardware work behind that date has actually kept pace. Booster 21 completed a full-duration, 33-engine static fire on August 28, producing roughly 9,240 tonnes of thrust at Starbase's Pad 2, after Ship 41 cleared its own single-engine and six-engine firings earlier in the month ([Tesla Oracle](https://www.teslaoracle.com/2026/09/02/fcc-filing-reveals-starship-flight-14-launch-on-september-15-spacex-conducts-33-engine-static-fire-on-booster-21/)). That is the last major ground test before flight. Flight 14 would be Starship's first genuine orbital-class mission and, if SpaceX goes ahead with it on this flight, the first attempt to catch a returning Ship with the launch tower's arms. September 15 falls just outside this newsletter's window, but the countdown toward it starts inside it.

## Blue Origin rebuilds one pad while starting a second

Nine days after May's static-fire explosion tore up Launch Complex 36, Blue Origin had the debris cleared and was rebuilding the pad, now redesignated LC-36A, along with the transporter-erector the blast also destroyed. The retrofit for the BE-4 oxygen valve blamed for the explosion was expected out of manufacturing by late August; there is no independent confirmation yet that it has cleared testing. Alongside that repair, construction is underway on LC-36B, a second pad built specifically for the larger New Glenn 9x4, with its own vertical integration and payload processing facilities going up nearby to support higher launch cadence later ([Space UpClose](https://www.spaceupclose.com/2026/08/blue-origin-begins-construction-of-new-launch-pad-lc-36b-while-rebuilding-lc-36a/)). CEO Dave Limp's standing target is a return to flight before the end of 2026 on the current 7x2 vehicle; that requires both a clean valve fix and a rebuilt pad, and neither is confirmed done yet.

## Artemis III: still no completion date on engines or boosters

Nothing changed on the ground at Kennedy Space Center this week that NASA has said publicly. The four RS-25 engines that started going into the Artemis III core stage on August 24 are still being installed in High Bay 2, and NASA has not announced when all four will be seated. The twin solid rocket booster segments, stacking since July, were more than halfway up the mobile launcher as of the last update. Both remain open questions carried over from last week rather than resolved this one.

## Five things that could move this week

Whether NASA and SpaceX name a new Crew-13 launch date, and whether it lands inside September or slips further. Any confirmation that Booster 21 and Ship 41 have finished stack integration ahead of the September 15 target. Independent word that Blue Origin's retrofit valve hardware has actually cleared testing, rather than just manufacturing. Whether satellite imagery or a company update shows real progress on LC-36B's foundations. And whether NASA says anything concrete about the RS-25 install or booster-stacking timeline for Artemis III, which has gone quiet for two weeks running.
