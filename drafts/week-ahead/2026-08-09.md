# The Week Ahead: August 10–16, 2026

A short briefing on what to watch in human lunar exploration and the surrounding launch market over the next seven days.

---

## What's actually launching

A routine week on the manifest, with one weather-delayed mission and one genuine first.

- Falcon 9 / Starlink 10-19: August 10, 14:49 UTC from Cape Canaveral SLC-40.
- H3-22 / QZS-7 (Michibiki No. 7): now targeting the early hours of August 10 in Japan, roughly 19:00 UTC on August 9, after Typhoon Dolphin forced JAXA to scrub the original August 7 attempt. A backup window runs into September if weather forces another slip ([JAXA](https://global.jaxa.jp/press/2026/08/20260807-2_e.html)).
- Zhuque-3 Flight 2: no earlier than August 10 from Jiuquan, though some trackers list it as late as August 31, so treat the date as soft. LandSpace is attempting its second orbital booster recovery after its first vehicle reached orbit in December but missed the catch ([Space.com](https://www.space.com/space-exploration/launches-spacecraft/chinese-company-landspace-targets-aug-10-for-historic-rocket-launch-and-landing-attempt)).
- Falcon 9 / Starlink 17-49: August 12 from Vandenberg SLC-4E.
- Falcon 9: August 15 from Vandenberg SLC-4E, payload not publicly disclosed as of this writing.
- Falcon 9 / Globalstar 2-R Mission 1: August 16, 01:12 UTC from Cape Canaveral, replenishing Globalstar's mobile satellite constellation.

None of this is Artemis hardware. That work is happening on the ground at Kennedy, at Starbase and at Cape Canaveral's rebuilt pad 36.

## Artemis 3: Orion becomes one spacecraft, engines wait for the stack

On July 30, NASA joined Orion's crew and service modules inside the Neil Armstrong Operations and Checkout Building at Kennedy Space Center, the point at which the vehicle that will carry astronauts Randy Bresnik, Andre Douglas, Frank Rubio and ESA's Luca Parmitano stops being two major assemblies and becomes one spacecraft ([NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/blogs/missions/2026/08/05/nasa-joins-artemis-iii-orion-modules-rocket-hardware-update/)). Power-up and integrated systems testing follow before Orion is ready to stack atop its rocket.

The other half of that pairing is close behind. All four RS-25 engines, built by L3Harris, reached the Vehicle Assembly Building on July 21 after shipping from Stennis Space Center. NASA says the final engine is due to join the core stage this summer, which puts the milestone inside this week's window without a firm date attached. Once that connection is made, the core stage can move between the already-stacked twin boosters, the step that turns individual pieces of hardware into a single rocket. Watch for NASA confirming that installation is complete.

## Starship: Flight 14 aims for the first tower catch

Flight 13 on July 24 was the program's cleanest outing yet: 20 Starlink V3 satellites deployed, an in-space Raptor relight, and a Ship that survived an Indian Ocean splashdown intact. The booster's landing burn lit five engines instead of thirteen and hit the water hard, and that remains unresolved.

Elon Musk said afterward that SpaceX would attempt to catch Ship with the launch tower's arms on Flight 14, alongside the vehicle's first genuine orbital profile. Reporting in early August put the target near the end of August, contingent on the FAA clearing the expanded landing-zone footprint a tower catch requires ([TechTimes](https://www.techtimes.com/articles/322289/20260730/starship-ship-catch-cleared-flight-14-attempt-first-orbital-return-tower.htm)). Treat any specific date as soft until that paperwork clears. The ship-to-ship propellant transfer demonstration that Artemis 4's lander actually depends on is a separate mission, still no earlier than late 2026.

## Blue Origin: the valve behind the explosion

Blue Origin CEO Dave Limp said this week that the May 28 New Glenn explosion traced to the main oxygen valve on one of the rocket's seven BE-4 first-stage engines ([Spaceflight Now](https://spaceflightnow.com/2026/08/06/blue-origin-identifies-engine-issue-behind-new-glenn-explosion/)). The fix is a retrofit to existing engines rather than a redesign, with updated hardware expected to be ready by the end of August and testing continuing at Stennis Space Center this fall. The company still says it wants New Glenn flying again before the end of the year.

The rebuild at Launch Complex 36 is moving in parallel, and Blue Origin is using the opportunity to shift toward a hybrid horizontal-and-vertical integration approach it wants to reuse across a future second pad, rather than simply restoring what exploded in May. Endurance, the Blue Moon Mk1 lander for NASA's first Moon Base mission, finished thermal vacuum testing at Johnson earlier this year. It is ready. It is still waiting on a rocket.

And whether LandSpace's second Zhuque-3 booster-catch attempt actually happens this week or slips toward the end of the month.
