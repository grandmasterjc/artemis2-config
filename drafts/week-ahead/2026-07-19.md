# The Week Ahead — July 20–26, 2026

A short briefing on what to watch in human lunar exploration and the surrounding launch market over the next seven days. This edition comes to you a day late — Thursday's Starship abort reshuffled more than one calendar.

---

## What's actually launching

- Falcon 9 / Starlink 17-39 — July 20, 14:00 UTC from Vandenberg SLC-4E.
- Starship Flight 13 (second attempt) — targeted for July 20, 22:45 UTC from Starbase, Texas, with backup opportunities later in the week ([Space.com](https://www.space.com/space-exploration/launches-spacecraft/spacex-starship-flight-13-launch-what-time)).
- Falcon 9 / MRV-1 — July 21, 21:15 UTC from Cape Canaveral SLC-40. Northrop Grumman/SpaceLogistics' Mission Robotic Vehicle-1, flying DARPA's robotic satellite-servicing payload.
- Gravity-1 (OrienSpace) — July 22, 02:50 UTC, sea launch from the Yellow Sea.
- Long March 3B/E — July 23, 11:00 UTC from Xichang (payload unconfirmed).
- Falcon 9 / Starlink 17-51 — July 24, 14:00 UTC from Vandenberg.

MRV-1 is the quiet one worth a look: a spacecraft designed to grab and service other satellites in orbit, which is the same family of capabilities — rendezvous, capture, robotic manipulation — the lunar logistics chain will eventually lean on.

## Starship: two new Raptors and a Monday-evening window

The week opens with the launch that did not happen last week. On Thursday, four Raptors failed to light at ignition and Flight 13 aborted on the pad — the first last-second abort for a full-scale Starship. SpaceX swapped two engines, and the retry is targeted for Monday at 22:45 UTC ([Space.com](https://www.space.com/news/live/spacex-starship-flight-13-launch-updates-july-17-2026)).

The scorecard to keep at hand when it flies, carried over from Flight 12 in May: does Super Heavy finally manage its controlled Gulf splashdown, does Ship repeat its on-target arrival off Western Australia, and does the in-space Raptor relight work this time? New on this flight: the first 20 Starlink V3 satellites deploy on the suborbital arc, six of them with cameras pointed back at Ship's heat shield during reentry.

For the Artemis schedule, the flight itself is the point. The propellant-transfer demonstration that Artemis IV's lander depends on is still penciled in for V3 hardware later this year, and it needs a launch cadence to exist. A clean Monday flight puts the program back on that path; another stand-down starts eating into the autumn.

## Artemis III: the stack gets taller

At Kennedy, booster stacking that began July 11 continues inside the Vehicle Assembly Building — the remaining segments of the twin solid rocket boosters go vertical on mobile launcher 1 over the coming weeks. The things to watch for this week are NASA imagery of new segment lifts, word on the remaining RS-25 engine installations on the core stage, and the monthly launch-countdown simulation cadence that Kennedy started in June.

Orion, meanwhile, enters the quieter phase after its heat-shield installation earlier this month: crew module and service module integration in the Armstrong Operations and Checkout Building. "Installed" milestones will keep coming; the interesting language to watch for is anything about integration test results.

## The other pad: LC-36's rebuild clock

Nothing is expected to launch from Blue Origin this week, and that is the story. The New Glenn return-to-flight — which the Moon Base I lander mission and the broader south-pole manifest are waiting on — is still targeted for late this year, with the LC-36 rebuild and the redesigned transporter-erector as the pacing items. Any construction-progress update out of the Cape this week feeds directly into whether the first-quarter-2027 target for Endurance holds.

## Five things that could move this week

- A second Starship stand-down, which would push Flight 13 toward August and compress the fall test calendar.
- NASA booster-stacking imagery from the VAB — each released photo is a de facto schedule update.
- MRV-1's early orbital operations. A clean start for DARPA's servicing demo matters to everyone planning in-space logistics.
- Any NASA response to June's OIG memo on canceled Artemis hardware — the agency's 30-day comment window has now closed.
- New Glenn pad-rebuild news from Blue Origin, in either direction.
