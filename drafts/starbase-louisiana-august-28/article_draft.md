SpaceX and Louisiana Governor Jeff Landry announced on August 25 that the company will build its largest launch site yet on the Gulf coast, a 125,000-acre (50,600-hectare) complex on Pecan Island in Vermilion Parish called Starbase Louisiana. The plan calls for five complexes of two Starship towers each, 10 pads in total, each with its own propellant farm, plus onsite methane production, power generation, a deepwater port to ship vehicles in from Texas, and vehicle processing buildings. Construction starts in 2027. The first launch is no earlier than 2029. The headline figure is $100 billion, which is 10 times the entire budget NASA has assigned to the first phase of its Moon Base program.

"Today is nothing short of a transformational moment," Landry said at the announcement. "Today is like an inflection point in time in Louisiana's history." SpaceX president Gwynne Shotwell described a "clean-sheet design," a self-sustaining spaceport with its own propellant production and power generation, sited after a seven-year search for somewhere coastal, away from population centers, with natural gas access and clear launch trajectories.

For anyone following Artemis, the interesting number is not the $100 billion. It is 2029. The reason a site like this matters to a moon program is launch cadence, because putting one Starship on the lunar surface requires filling a propellant depot in orbit first, and current estimates put that at 10 to 16 tanker flights per landing. Starbase Louisiana is the industrial answer to that problem. It also arrives, on SpaceX's own schedule, after the landing it would serve.

## Why a launch site is an Artemis problem at all

Starship does not fly to the moon on the propellant it launches with. The architecture NASA bought requires a depot placed in Earth orbit, then a series of tanker Starships that fill it, and only then a fueled lander that departs for the surface. Public estimates of how many tanker flights that takes range from 10 to 16 for a single landing, and the number is not settled because the demonstration that would establish it has not flown.

That demonstration is the ship-to-ship propellant transfer test. NASA's Office of Inspector General (OIG) reported in March that it had already slipped from its original March 2025 target to March 2026. That revised date has now passed too. Until it flies, every figure downstream of it, including how many launches per landing and therefore how much pad capacity the program needs, is an estimate rather than a measurement.

This is the gap Starbase Louisiana is built to close. Ten pads with dedicated propellant farms, fed by methane made on site, is a plausible way to fly the tanker cadence that one lunar landing consumes. Starbase Texas was not designed for that volume, which is a large part of why the company spent seven years looking for somewhere else.

## How 2029 compares with the Artemis calendar

Line the dates up. Artemis 3 flies next year as a low Earth orbit rehearsal, after NASA restructured it out of the landing business in February. The crewed landing moved to Artemis 4, no earlier than 2028. Construction at Pecan Island begins in 2027, and nothing launches from it before 2029.

On those dates, the spaceport built for lunar launch cadence opens at least a year after the mission that needs the cadence. Neither SpaceX nor the state framed the announcement around Artemis, and it is worth being precise about why that is reasonable: Starbase Louisiana is plainly aimed at Starlink and at whatever a company that has just gone public wants to tell investors about launch volume. A moon landing is not its business case.

But the timing does say something about how SpaceX reads its own Artemis obligations. A company that expected to be flying a dozen tanker missions per landing in 2028 would not schedule the facility for that cadence to open in 2029. Either the landing is expected to happen with the pads that already exist, or the landing is not expected in 2028.

## What the site would be built on

The location is not empty ground. Three conservation groups, the Louisiana Wildlife Federation, the National Wildlife Federation and the Pontchartrain Conservancy, filed comments describing the area as "136,000 acres of coastal marsh in Vermilion Parish, among the most productive wildlife habitat on the continent." The marshes, they wrote, "shelter species of extreme conservation concern including the whooping crane," a bird reintroduced to Louisiana only in 2011, and "support wintering populations of ducks and geese numbering in the millions."

Louisiana is also losing this ground on its own. Its coast is eroding faster than any other in the country, and researchers project that 75 percent of its remaining wetlands will be gone by 2070. Those marshes are what currently absorbs storm surge on this stretch of coast. Hurricane Laura came ashore two parishes west of Pecan Island in August 2020 as a category 4.

Running underneath the local fight is a federal one. The Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) has proposed rule changes that would waive requirements under 13 environmental and public health laws for commercial space operations, among them the Endangered Species Act, the Clean Water Act and the Clean Air Act. The conservation groups' comments were filed against that proposal, and they argued the effect "is not abstract. It is immediate and local."

SpaceX says it has worked with the Louisiana Department of Wildlife and Fisheries to address potential impacts to wildlife and fisheries habitat, and that any unavoidable wetland damage will be fully mitigated by the company.

## The jobs numbers do not agree with each other

The economics came with a spread rather than a figure. The state cited 3,000 permanent jobs. Musk, in a video played at the announcement, promised "north of 10,000 really exciting jobs to Louisiana." Shotwell's number was closer to Musk's. Vermilion Parish is to receive $25 million a year, growing over time, which the state estimated at roughly $820 million across 25 years.

A three-fold disagreement about headcount on the day of the announcement matters mostly because the $100 billion sits in the same category. It is a figure released jointly by a company and a governor at an economic development event, covering a build-out that runs past 2029, and nothing in the announcement breaks it into committed spending.

## What is still undecided

The propellant transfer demonstration has no confirmed date. It is the test that would turn the tanker estimate into a requirement, because once it flies, how much pad capacity a landing consumes stops being arithmetic and becomes a measurement. Nothing about Louisiana's capacity can be sized against Artemis until then.

Starship's own schedule has meanwhile done what it usually does. Flight 14, the first attempt to reach orbit, was filed with the Federal Communications Commission for late August, then pushed after Isaacman said publicly he expected early September, and trackers now point at the end of that month. It had not flown as of this writing.

The regulatory question is the one with a deadline attached. Whether the FAA's proposed exclusion survives will decide how much environmental review Pecan Island gets before ground breaks in 2027, and the conservation groups have already filed against it. NASA has not commented on Starbase Louisiana at all, though its landing schedule depends on the cadence the site is meant to supply.

On its own terms, the August 25 announcement was exactly what it looked like: the biggest private launch complex anyone has planned, in a state that wants it, from a company that can now raise the money. What it is not is a fix for the 2028 landing. On the dates both parties published, the pads that would fly a dozen tankers per moon mission open the year after the moon mission.
