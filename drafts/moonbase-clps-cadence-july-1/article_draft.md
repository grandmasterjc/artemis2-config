NASA announced Tuesday that it has awarded nearly $600 million for four additional commercial lunar deliveries in late 2028: two missions to Astrobotic ($297.9 million combined), one to Firefly Aerospace ($144.2 million), and one to Intuitive Machines ($148.3 million). (NASA, June 30, 2026)

On paper, this is another round of Commercial Lunar Payload Services buys: NASA pays for rides, companies deliver instruments, and the agency gets surface data without owning the landers.

The interesting part is the payload bundle NASA chose to fly on every one of these missions, because it reads like a checklist of the Moon Base program's near-term worries: dust, navigation, and radiation.

NASA is also signaling it wants cadence: order missions faster, launch more often, and treat the Moon as something you learn by repetition rather than by one-off hero landings.

## The track record that explains the strategy

Repetition is not an abstract virtue here. Four CLPS missions have flown so far, and only one — Firefly's Blue Ghost Mission 1 in 2025 — was a complete success. Astrobotic's Peregrine never reached the Moon after a propulsion failure in 2024, and both of Intuitive Machines' landers (IM-1 in 2024, IM-2 in 2025) reached the surface but tipped over, completing only some of their objectives.

That is the context for these awards. All three companies will fly updated versions of already-flown lander designs, and NASA's bet is that iteration — not fresh starts — is what turns a 25 percent full-success rate into routine delivery.

NASA's acting director of cargo landers, Ryan Stephan, described the strategy as a proving ground: "We're building a proving ground for Moon Base operations." (NASA, June 30, 2026)

He added why cadence matters: "Accelerating our Moon mission ordering cadence and launch opportunities enable us to move quickly to learn, iterate, and improve." (NASA, June 30, 2026)

That is the basic Moon Base transition. If you want a sustained outpost, you need deliveries to feel routine.

## Why NASA is flying the same three payloads four times

Each of the four deliveries will carry the same three NASA payloads. Flying identical instruments across different landers, engine designs, propellants, and landing profiles lets NASA compare results directly — the closest thing lunar operations has to a controlled experiment.

The bundle is:

- SCALPSS (Stereo Camera for Lunar Plume Surface Studies): a camera array intended to produce a 3D view of how an engine's exhaust plume interacts with lunar dust during descent. (NASA, June 30, 2026)
- LRA (Laser Retroreflector Array): a passive reflector used by orbiters or landers to determine position or navigate using laser ranging. (NASA, June 30, 2026)
- LETS (Linear Energy Transfer Spectrometer): a radiation monitor that measures the energy carried by incoming space radiation at the surface. (NASA, June 30, 2026)

NASA's description of SCALPSS is unusually specific about the operational goal: improve models that predict dust erosion and ejecta as bigger and heavier spacecraft and hardware are delivered near each other. (NASA, June 30, 2026)

That is not a hypothetical concern. If the program expects super-heavy vehicles and multiple surface elements at the south pole, plume effects and dust become a programmatic risk: visibility, hardware abrasion, contamination, and potentially damage to nearby assets.

LRA is the other half of making landings routine. NASA describes it as a small passive device that requires no power, intended to build a network of permanent location markers for future exploration. (NASA, June 30, 2026)

LETS is the long-duration piece. It provides the detailed radiation data needed to design safer missions, protect astronauts, and plan long-duration exploration. (NASA, June 30, 2026)

Put together, the trio maps to three prerequisites for sustained operations:

- land repeatedly without creating self-inflicted hazards
- establish stable location references you can come back to
- characterize the environment crews and electronics have to live in

The visual framing here is telling: the lander is depicted as infrastructure, not as a one-time stunt. That is how NASA is increasingly talking about the program — the agency has even begun rebranding earlier CLPS missions under a new "Moon Base" mission line.

## The Earthside bottleneck: launch infrastructure

Cadence on the Moon depends on cadence on Earth.

A June 22 audit from NASA's Office of Inspector General warns that the agency's launch infrastructure at Kennedy Space Center and Wallops is dated and often does not provide the capacity to meet growing demand from NASA and its partners. (NASA OIG, June 22, 2026)

Even if commercial landers and payloads are ready, shared ground systems can become the schedule. In a Moon Base era, the limiting factor may be mundane: power distribution, commodity pipelines, transportation networks, and available pad time.

## What to watch next

The June 30 release also tees up the next layer of Moon Base work.

NASA says it is considering sending PROMISE, described as a hybrid engineering development version of the Mars Perseverance and Curiosity rovers, to characterize the lunar surface and subsurface and prospect for resources. (NASA, June 30, 2026)

NASA also plans to solicit proposals for landers to deliver a power and avionics technology demonstration, another science manifest, and a south pole optical imager, and says it will seek a lunar communication and navigation relay constellation. (NASA, June 30, 2026)

Where this leaves us is straightforward: NASA is paying for repetition and operational learning, and it is doing it with instruments that look boring until you realize they are the scaffolding for human-scale surface operations.
