On April 10, the Orion capsule Integrity hit the top of Earth's atmosphere at nearly 25,000 miles per hour (40,000 kilometers per hour) and came down in the Pacific within 2.9 miles (4.7 kilometers) of its target. Artemis 2 had carried four astronauts around the moon and back in nine days. According to the National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA), the heat shield lost far less charred material than it did on the uncrewed Artemis 1 flight.

A clean return does not mean the road to a landing is clear. NASA's Aerospace Safety Advisory Panel (ASAP), the independent group that reviews the agency's human spaceflight safety, recommended that NASA "Re-examine the mission objectives and system architecture for Artemis III and subsequent missions to establish a more balanced approach to risk." This article goes through the five risks that come up most often in the public record, from NASA's Office of Inspector General (OIG), the ASAP and Congress, and explains for each what the problem is and what is being done. They are ordered by how much remains untested, with the best-tested last.

## Two landers

No astronaut has ever flown on either lander NASA has chosen. SpaceX's Starship human landing system (HLS) stands about 165 feet (50 meters) tall and carries an elevator to lower crew to the surface. Blue Origin's Blue Moon is the second provider, designed for longer stays and more cargo. NASA's HLS page says SpaceX must fly one uncrewed demonstration before any crew rides its lander to the surface.

The agency changed its plan this year to make room for that testing. In a May 13 release, NASA said Artemis 3 will stay in Earth orbit. Four astronauts will dock Orion with pathfinder versions of both landers, and they may enter at least one of them. Jeremy Parsons of NASA's Moon to Mars program called it "an important stepping stone to successfully landing on the Moon with Artemis IV," the flight that will carry crews to the south pole region.

The mitigation is to find problems in Earth orbit first, where a fault costs a delay and not a rescue. Starship reached orbit for the first time on Sept. 28, on its 14th flight. Neither lander has docked with Orion.

## Refueling in orbit

A Starship cannot lift enough propellant from Earth to fly to the moon and land. It has to be filled in orbit by other Starships acting as tankers. Estimates cited by NASA and SpaceX run to about 10 tanker launches for a single lunar mission, and the true figure depends on how much propellant boils away between launches.

The propellants are liquid oxygen at about minus 297 degrees Fahrenheit (minus 183 degrees Celsius) and liquid methane at about minus 258 degrees Fahrenheit (minus 161 degrees Celsius). Moving them between two ships, in weightlessness, where liquid floats free inside the tank, has never been done at this scale. SpaceX's ship-to-ship transfer demonstration was first planned for March 2025, then for March 2026. It has not flown and is not on a published schedule.

NASA's mitigation is to require the demonstration before it commits a crew. A NASA program manager has said the agency wants autonomous transfer flights repeated more than once or twice before crewed missions proceed. Each test measures slosh, tank pressure and boil-off, the numbers that settle how many tankers a landing needs.

## The moon suit

The OIG published report IG-26-006 on April 20, on how NASA bought its next-generation spacesuits. Two companies won contracts in 2022. NASA and Collins Aerospace removed Collins' task orders in 2024, citing its inability to meet the space station demonstration schedule, and that left Axiom Space as the only supplier.

One supplier means no fallback. The OIG compared six earlier NASA programs and found that the time from contract award to first flight averaged 8.7 years. Axiom says its Axiom Extravehicular Mobility Unit (AxEMU) will support moonwalks of at least eight hours, including time inside permanently shadowed craters. The suit has not yet been worn in space.

The suit is a third critical path, separate from the rocket and the lander.

## Money through December

Artemis competes for money every year, against science, aeronautics and the space station. The White House asked Congress for $18.829 billion for NASA in fiscal 2027, down from $24.438 billion. The House Appropriations Committee rejected the cut in May and kept NASA flat, according to SpacePolicyOnline, with $2.277 billion for landers.

Congress then passed a continuing resolution, signed on Sept. 2, that holds NASA at its 2026 level through Dec. 11. A stopgap does not fund anything new, but it removes the immediate threat of a large cut. The next decision point is that December date.

Money also shapes the architecture. NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman, responding to the ASAP's recommendation, said the agency is "recalibrating our acquisition strategy" and "accelerating proposals for human landing systems to preserve schedule margin" for a landing by 2028.

## The heat shield

Orion's shield is made of a material called Avcoat, which burns away slowly as the capsule slows from lunar-return speed. On Artemis 1, in 2022, NASA's Inspector General found that char wore away in more than 100 places.

After more than 100 ground tests in fall 2024, NASA reported the cause: the shield did not let enough of the gas generated inside the Avcoat escape, so pieces cracked and broke off. For Artemis 2 the agency changed the entry trajectory. For later shields it changed manufacturing to give the material uniform permeability.

The April result supports the fix. NASA's first assessment, published April 20, found char loss significantly reduced in both quantity and size and said entry speed matched predictions to within one mile per hour. X-ray scans and sample analysis at Marshall Space Flight Center continue. Of the five risks, it is the only one with a flight record behind it.

## Back to the capsule

The heat shield was the hardest thing to see from the outside on April 10, and it was the thing that worked. The other four are different: they are hardware that has not yet flown in the form astronauts will use, or money that has to be voted every year. NASA's plan answers each with a test before a crew depends on it, starting with the Artemis 3 docking flight, and the order of those tests now sets the order of the schedule.
