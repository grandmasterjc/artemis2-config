On Oct. 9, 2009, NASA crashed a spent Centaur rocket stage into Cabeus, a crater near the moon's south pole whose floor lies in permanent shadow. The impact dug a hole about 66 feet (20 meters) across and threw roughly 350 metric tons of lunar soil up into the sunlight, according to NASA. A small spacecraft trailing minutes behind, the Lunar Crater Observation and Sensing Satellite (LCROSS), flew through the debris before it hit the surface too. In the plume was water: about 155 kilograms (342 pounds) of vapor and ice in the instruments' field of view, the mission team reported in the journal Science a year later.

Apollo had found nothing like it. All six landings touched down within about 26 degrees of the lunar equator, and the soil the crews brought home was dry. Artemis astronauts are headed almost as far from those sites as the moon allows. Why not go back to where Apollo landed? A 2022 NASA white paper, "Why Artemis Will Focus on the Lunar South Polar Region," answers with the lighting, the ice and the age of the rock. The first two come from how little the moon is tilted.

## A 1.5-degree tilt

Earth's axis leans 23.5 degrees, which gives the planet its seasons and its polar summers. The moon's equator is inclined just 1.54 degrees to the plane of its path around the sun, according to the white paper. Seen from the lunar poles, the sun never climbs overhead. It circles the horizon, never rising more than a few degrees above it and sometimes sinking out of sight.

Low sunlight on rough ground makes long shadows, and inside a deep enough crater the shadow never lifts. Shackleton crater sits almost exactly on the south pole. It is 13 miles (21 kilometers) wide and more than 2.5 miles (4 kilometers) deep, according to the Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter Camera (LROC) team at Arizona State University.

Its rim catches the sun. Its floor never does.

The moon has no air to carry warmth in from the sunlit ground nearby, so these permanently shadowed regions are among the coldest places on its surface. NASA puts some of them as low as minus 334 degrees Fahrenheit (minus 203 degrees Celsius). A water molecule that lands on ground that cold stays there. Such places are called cold traps.

## Ice in the cold traps

The idea is older than Apollo. In 1961, Kenneth Watson and colleagues proposed that water ice would persist in lunar craters that never receive sunlight, NASA's history of the search recounts.

Orbiters later agreed. In 1998, Lunar Prospector found the moon's largest concentrations of hydrogen in the areas never exposed to sunlight.

LCROSS was the direct test. From the water in the plume, a team led by Anthony Colaprete of NASA's Ames Research Center estimated that the soil at the impact site was 5.6 percent water ice by mass, give or take 2.9 percentage points. At the central figure, a metric ton of that soil holds 56 kilograms of water, or about 15 gallons.

In 2018, a team led by Shuai Li of the University of Hawaii reported ice lying exposed on the surface at both poles. They found it in light scattered into shadowed craters and recorded by NASA's Moon Mineralogy Mapper, an instrument on India's Chandrayaan-1 orbiter.

The deposits are patchy. Only about 3.5 percent of the cold traps showed exposed ice, the team found, and its modeling put the richest spots at about 30 percent ice by weight, mixed into the soil. At the south pole most of it is concentrated in craters. In the north it is spread wider and thinner, according to NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory.

Nobody knows yet where the water came from. The white paper lists comets and asteroids, the moon's own interior, and the solar wind reacting with the soil, and says only samples in laboratories on Earth will sort out the history.

The practical interest is simpler. Water is heavy to launch, and ice dug up on the moon would supply a crew with water and oxygen. Split into hydrogen and oxygen, it is also rocket propellant.

## Ridges in near-constant sunlight

The same low sun that leaves crater floors dark keeps high ground lit. At the Apollo sites, about 14.5 Earth days of daylight are followed by 14.5 days of night. Near the pole, parts of a ridge running between Shackleton and de Gerlache craters are illuminated up to 90 percent of the time, according to the LROC team. No spot is lit permanently.

For hardware, that means solar panels that produce power through most of the year and smaller batteries to get through the dark. The white paper calls these places "oases of light."

## Older ground than Apollo's

The south pole sits on the edge of the South Pole-Aitken basin, a scar more than 1,550 miles (2,500 kilometers) wide that NASA describes as the largest and oldest impact basin in the solar system. Surfaces in the Artemis exploration zone are older than about 3.85 billion years, older than any Apollo site, according to the white paper.

Dating the basin would show when the largest collisions of the early solar system happened. It is also a reason the paper gives for choosing the south pole over the north: being close to the basin raises the odds of collecting pieces of it.

"The Moon's South Pole is a completely different environment than where we landed during the Apollo missions," Sarah Noble, NASA's Artemis lunar science lead, said in October 2024. "It offers access to some of the Moon's oldest terrain, as well as cold, shadowed regions that may contain water and other compounds."

## Rough terrain, long shadows

None of this makes the pole easy. Shackleton's upper walls often slope more than 30 degrees, according to the LROC team, and the low sun stretches the shadow of every boulder.

![Athena on its side on Mons Mouton, March 6, 2025. Credit: Intuitive Machines.](https://grandmasterjc.github.io/artemis2-config/updates/images/lunar-south-pole-november-18-athena.jpg)

Two robotic landers show the difference a few hundred miles make. India's Chandrayaan-3 landed upright on Aug. 23, 2023, at about 69 degrees south, still some 390 miles (625 kilometers) from the pole. Intuitive Machines' Athena reached Mons Mouton, far closer to the pole, on March 6, 2025, and ended up on its side. The company's chief executive, Steve Altemus, later named long shadows from the low sun among the causes.

![NASA's nine candidate landing regions, with a star at the south pole. Credit: NASA.](https://grandmasterjc.github.io/artemis2-config/updates/images/lunar-south-pole-november-18-landing-regions.jpg)

In October 2024, NASA named nine candidate landing regions, judged on terrain, lighting, communication with Earth, science potential and launch windows. The list was drawn up when Artemis 3 was the landing mission. That landing has since moved to Artemis 4, planned for 2028.

## Scouts still on Earth

Everything known about the ice comes from orbit and from one impact plume. The missions meant to check it on the ground have yet to launch.

NASA's Volatiles Investigating Polar Exploration Rover (VIPER) carries a 1-meter (3.3-foot) drill to map ice at Mons Mouton, and NASA said in September 2025 that a Blue Origin Blue Moon lander would deliver it. An earlier Blue Moon lander, Endurance, is bound for the Shackleton ridge. Both ride on New Glenn, which has not flown since one exploded on its pad during a test in May 2026. China's Chang'e-7, built to hunt for ice near Shackleton, missed its launch window in August, and Chinese officials have announced no new date.

Seventeen years after the Centaur hit Cabeus, that plume remains the closest any instrument has come to the ice in a cold trap. The first rover or astronaut to drill into a cold trap will find out how much there is and how hard it is to get out.
