Artemis 3's rocket is getting its engines. The National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA) [reported on Sept. 1](https://www.nasa.gov/image-article/historic-engines-take-their-place-on-artemis-iii/) that technicians at Kennedy Space Center in Florida had begun installing the four RS-25 engines on Aug. 24. The Space Launch System (SLS) is being assembled for a 2027 mission that will keep its crew in Earth orbit.

Under [NASA's published flight plan](https://www.nasa.gov/directorates/esdmd/artemis-campaign-development-division/human-landing-system-program/how-nasas-artemis-iii-lander-test-will-pave-way-for-moon-landings/), Orion is to dock with a SpaceX Starship test vehicle. "Astronauts will not enter the Starship test lander during Artemis III," the agency says. During the same mission, up to two crew members are expected to enter Blue Origin's prototype.

That difference defines what the rehearsal can establish. NASA's [mission outline](https://www.nasa.gov/missions/artemis/artemis-3/nasa-outlines-preliminary-artemis-iii-mission-plans/) assigns Artemis 3 Orion's first docking-system demonstration and a longer evaluation of its life support. The flight will test whether spacecraft work together, a necessary capability for a lunar expedition. It won't replace the [uncrewed landing demonstration](https://www.nasa.gov/reference/human-landing-systems/) NASA requires before a crewed touchdown.

## Two different prototypes

SpaceX plans to use a Starship Version 3 with a docking system added to its nose. NASA describes tests of how the 171-foot (52-meter) spacecraft behaves while joined to Orion.

Blue Origin's vehicle is based on the company's Mark 2 crew lander architecture. Up to two astronauts are expected to enter it wearing their orange Orion crew survival system suits. Steve Creech, program manager of NASA's Human Landing System (HLS) Program at Marshall Space Flight Center, [described the prototypes' objectives](https://www.nasa.gov/directorates/esdmd/artemis-campaign-development-division/human-landing-system-program/how-nasas-artemis-iii-lander-test-will-pave-way-for-moon-landings/) as complementary to uncrewed demonstrations at the moon.

## The docking interface

Engineers already have evidence that Starship's docking hardware works on the ground. In [February 2024, NASA reported](https://www.nasa.gov/image-article/nasa-spacex-test-starship-lunar-lander-docking-system/) that it and SpaceX had put a full-scale system through more than 200 docking scenarios over 10 days at Johnson Space Center in Houston. The tests varied approach angles and speeds to simulate contact between spacecraft.

NASA said the hardware demonstrated an initial capture while playing the active role in docking. Its mechanisms attached to the passive target, and the results supplied data to validate computer models. Those tests exercised the connection hardware. They did not put Orion and Starship together in space.

Brian Anderson, Orion's rendezvous, proximity operations and docking manager, explained the reason for flight testing in a [separate NASA account](https://www.nasa.gov/humans-in-space/key-test-drive-of-orion-on-nasas-artemis-ii-to-aid-future-missions/): ground simulations and actual spacecraft behavior always differ. Artemis 3 gives engineers a chance to compare their predictions with the performance of the vehicles in orbit. The flight will exercise the spacecraft as a connected system.

## Apollo's orbital rehearsal

NASA reached for an Apollo comparison when it [announced the revised Artemis sequence in February](https://www.nasa.gov/news-release/nasa-adds-mission-to-artemis-lunar-program-updates-architecture/). Associate Administrator Amit Kshatriya called for successive flights that build capability without taking unnecessarily large steps in risk.

Apollo 9 supplies the precedent. It launched on March 3, 1969, after Apollo 8 had already carried astronauts into lunar orbit. The next crew stayed around Earth to test the lunar module. Returning to Earth orbit was part of the route to a landing.

The hardware made that flight valuable. According to [NASA's Apollo 9 mission history](https://www.nasa.gov/humans-in-space/apollo-9-crew-completes-first-command-and-lunar-module-docking-in-space/), astronauts flew the lunar module separately from the command and service module. On March 7, they discarded its descent stage and fired the ascent-stage engine in space.

Artemis 3's planned Starship encounter covers less of the lander's operation. Apollo 9 put astronauts inside their lander and tested the propulsion needed for its return from the surface. NASA's [Artemis 3 overview](https://www.nasa.gov/mission/artemis-iii/) describes rendezvous and docking operations. The Apollo comparison supports testing near Earth, and it also shows how much of a lunar lander can remain untested by a docking alone.

## Upper-stage hardware

The flight also raises a practical question: does using a moon rocket for an Earth-orbit rehearsal consume an upper stage needed for a lunar mission? NASA's design gives a clear answer.

Orion's European-built service module will circularize the spacecraft's orbit after launch, according to the agency's [May mission outline](https://www.nasa.gov/missions/artemis/artemis-3/nasa-outlines-preliminary-artemis-iii-mission-plans/). The rocket won't carry an interim cryogenic propulsion stage. In its place NASA will fly "a spacer without propulsion capabilities," matching the upper stage's overall dimensions and interface connection points.

That powered upper stage cannot be expended on Artemis 3 because it won't be aboard. NASA also says the orbit "increases overall mission success by allowing more launch opportunities for each element as compared to a lunar mission."

Preserving the stage still leaves a substantial launch campaign. NASA's [August processing report](https://www.nasa.gov/blogs/missions/2026/08/27/nasa-starts-artemis-iii-engine-install-boosters-crew-training-advance/) describes assembly of both solid rocket boosters alongside the core-stage engine installation. The resource question concerns the hardware and work assigned to this flight. The lunar upper stage is excluded from that bill.

## Remaining landing tests

A successful Artemis 3 would leave a major requirement outstanding. Under its Human Landing System contract, [NASA states](https://www.nasa.gov/reference/human-landing-systems/), "SpaceX will perform one uncrewed demonstration mission prior to use of their system on the Artemis III mission." That demonstration puts Starship on the moon. Docking in Earth orbit does not satisfy it.

SpaceX's propellant handling presents another test. The Government Accountability Office (GAO), in its [2026 assessment of NASA's major projects](https://files.gao.gov/reports/GAO-26-108556/index.html) published in July, found that SpaceX "has not yet demonstrated a critical technology it is developing to store and transfer propellant while in orbit, which is necessary for it to execute its plan for landing astronauts on the moon." Joining a lander to Orion does not demonstrate the ability to fill that lander for its lunar journey.

NASA's Office of Inspector General (OIG) also identified unfinished safety work in its [March lander audit](https://oig.nasa.gov/news/artemis-lander-program-faces-schedule-delays-and-unmitigated-crew-safety-risks/). The watchdog found that "gaps remain in its testing posture and crew survival analyses," and that if the landers hit a catastrophic event, NASA "would not have the capability to rescue stranded astronauts from space or the lunar surface." It also found the firm-fixed-price approach had held cost growth to 6 percent at SpaceX and under 1 percent at Blue Origin, while both providers had faced schedule delays.

Those findings concern readiness for a crewed lunar landing. A docking demonstration supplies evidence about one operation in that mission. Separate evidence is needed for the lander's other functions, including its ability to reach the surface safely.

## Flight preparations

Assembly is advancing on the crew's spacecraft as well as its rocket. NASA [reported that Orion's crew and service modules were joined on July 30](https://www.nasa.gov/blogs/missions/2026/08/05/nasa-joins-artemis-iii-orion-modules-rocket-hardware-update/). The crew module will accommodate the astronauts, and the service module supplies power and propulsion.

By its [late-August progress report](https://www.nasa.gov/blogs/missions/2026/08/27/nasa-starts-artemis-iii-engine-install-boosters-crew-training-advance/), NASA had powered up the joined spacecraft to check electrical connectivity and communications between the modules. The astronauts had begun integrated simulations with flight controllers, rehearsing mission timelines and responses to problems.

The engines featured in September's update are therefore being installed for a flight with a defined engineering purpose. Artemis 3 can establish how Orion operates with commercial lander prototypes. The [uncrewed landing demonstration](https://www.nasa.gov/reference/human-landing-systems/) will still have to happen afterwards, even if every planned docking succeeds.
