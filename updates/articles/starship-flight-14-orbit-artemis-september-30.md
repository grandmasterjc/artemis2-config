At 8:48 a.m. Eastern time on Sept. 28, a Starship lifted off from Starbase, Texas. About 22 minutes later, flight controllers called it "go for orbit." For the first time in 14 flights, the ship did not fly an arc that dropped back into the ocean. It stayed up, roughly 170 miles (275 kilometers) above Earth, and circled the planet.

It did so with one of its three vacuum-optimized Raptor engines dead. The flight ended early, after 3 hours, 8 minutes and 32 seconds, and the ship broke apart six seconds after splashdown in the Pacific. SpaceX still called it a success. What does a first orbit change for the National Aeronautics and Space Administration's (NASA) plan to land astronauts on the moon in 2028?

## The flight itself

Flight 14 carried the first operational payload Starship has flown: 26 Starlink V3 satellites, all released by about 10 a.m. SpaceX communications manager Dan Huot said the deployment equaled "20 Falcon 9 flights in terms of bandwidth." Elon Musk posted that the "first orbital flight of Starship" was successful.

The ship lost a vacuum Raptor shortly after separating from the Super Heavy booster. Controllers paused the orbital insertion, checked the data, and concluded the remaining engines could still finish the job, including the burn needed to come back down. The booster lost one of its 33 engines about a minute after liftoff and splashed down in the Gulf of Mexico. Because of the engine loss, SpaceX ended the mission early. The plan had been for about six orbits.

Huot acknowledged the ending was rougher than the previous flight's. "Most importantly, Starship made it to orbit," he said.

## Orbital speed

All 13 earlier flights were suborbital. A ship on that path is moving fast, but not fast enough to stay up. Orbit requires about 17,500 miles per hour (28,000 kilometers per hour), and whatever reaches it has to survive the trip down again.

The distinction matters because NASA's next crewed mission depends on it. Artemis 3 will stay in Earth orbit. According to [NASA's mission outline](https://www.nasa.gov/missions/artemis/artemis-3/nasa-outlines-preliminary-artemis-iii-mission-plans/), the four-person Orion crew will rendezvous and dock with test versions of the commercial landers from SpaceX and Blue Origin. Jeremy Parsons, acting assistant deputy administrator for Moon to Mars at NASA, said the flight will "help us learn how Orion, the crew, and ground teams all interact together" before astronauts go to the surface on Artemis 4. A Starship that could not reach orbit could not take part in that rendezvous at all.

## Refueling and the lander demo

Flight 14 answers a narrow question: can a Starship get into a stable orbit and come back under control? The harder questions come next.

A Starship that lands astronauts on the moon has to be filled with propellant in Earth orbit first, because one launch cannot lift a fully fueled ship all the way there. A second ship must transfer super-cold liquid oxygen and methane to the first, in weightlessness. That transfer of cryogenic propellant between spacecraft has not yet been shown in flight.

NASA also requires SpaceX to fly an uncrewed demonstration before a crew rides the lander, the agency says on its [human landing systems page](https://www.nasa.gov/reference/human-landing-systems/). That demonstration comes after orbit and after refueling. Flight 14 is the first of those steps, and so far the only one that has flown.

## The spacesuit

Even a flawless Starship would not put anyone on the surface without a suit. In April, NASA's Office of Inspector General (OIG) [published an audit](https://oig.nasa.gov/audits/nasas-acquisition-of-next-generation-spacesuit-services/), report IG-26-006, on how NASA bought its next-generation suits. Collins Aerospace dropped out of the program in 2024, leaving Axiom Space as the only supplier. The OIG warned that if Axiom cannot deliver in a timely way, NASA could be forced to change its lunar plans. Comparing six earlier NASA programs, it found that contract award to first flight averaged 8.7 years, a pattern that would put the lunar suit around mid-2031 if it held.

## Whether the timeline holds

NASA's target is a crewed landing on Artemis 4 in 2028, following a December 2025 executive order that set that goal. The case that it can hold is real. The Space Launch System (SLS) and Orion for Artemis 3 are being assembled on schedule at Kennedy Space Center, and Administrator Jared Isaacman said in August he was "extremely confident" the mission would fly in 2027. Flight 14 also cleared a requirement that had been open for 13 flights.

The case against is longer. In August, Isaacman told reporters that a Starship launch every couple of weeks through the end of the year would make him feel "very good" about SpaceX's contribution to Artemis 3. Flight 14 slipped from its early-September target to Sept. 28, and by our count came 65 days after Flight 13 on July 25. The orbital refueling test and the uncrewed landing have not flown, and they must happen before any crew lands. The suit, in the inspector general's analysis, is further from ready than the rocket. Each of those is a different organization's schedule, and the 2028 date needs all of them to land in sequence with no slips.

Taken together, the public record leaves 2028 very little margin, though it does not rule the date out. One flight, even a successful one, removes only one of the open items.
