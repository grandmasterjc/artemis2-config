NASA held its first dedicated Moon Base news conference Tuesday afternoon at agency headquarters in Washington. Administrator Jared Isaacman, science chief Dr. Lori Glaze, and Moon Base program executive Carlos Garcia Guillen spent an hour doing something the agency had not done before: putting names, dates, and contract awards behind what had so far been mostly architecture slides.

The headline is simple. Three lunar landers are flying to the south pole before the end of this calendar year, and NASA has now publicly tied each of them to the Moon Base program rather than to the older CLPS framing alone. The first, Moon Base 1, is the Blue Origin Mark One endurance lander, targeting Shackleton Connecting Ridge "no earlier than fall 2026." The second, Moon Base 2, is the Astrobotic Griffin lander carrying more than 500 kilograms of cargo, including the Astrolab FLIP rover. The third, Moon Base 3, launches in the same window with a science payload selected through NASA's PRISM program.

That is more activity than the lunar surface has seen in any twelve-month period since 1972, and it is happening before Artemis III even leaves the pad.

The second thing the briefing made clear is that the Moon Base is not a future program. Phase one — which Garcia Guillen described as already underway — runs from now through 2029 and is scoped for 25 launches, 21 landings, and roughly four metric tons of cargo delivered to the surface. Phase two scales to permanent infrastructure. Phase three is permanent habitation.

## A program that suddenly has a manifest

The Moon Base has existed as a concept since the Ignition Day rollout earlier this year, but Tuesday was the first time NASA presented it as a procurement schedule rather than a vision. The three 2026 missions read like a deliberate de-risking sequence.

Moon Base 1 carries two NASA science payloads — SCALPSS and a lunar retroreflector array — but its real job is "to demonstrate critical capabilities that reduce risk for the human landing system missions," Isaacman said. Translation: prove the Blue Origin Mark One can survive a south pole landing before astronauts ride a derivative of it.

Moon Base 2's Griffin lander is a cargo proof of concept. The 500-plus kilograms of payload includes the Astrolab FLIP rover, and the mission is framed as maturing capabilities for "future lunar terrain vehicle autonomous operations, logistics, and especially the LTV astronaut mobility." This is the dress rehearsal for the rovers Apollo never had at scale.

Moon Base 3 is the science-anchored mission, with payloads selected through PRISM — NASA's open-competition program that pulls in universities and industry. The mission is positioned to expand scientific understanding of the lunar surface in parallel with the engineering demos.

## The LTV award is the structural news

If the three landers are the visible 2026 deliverables, the Lunar Terrain Vehicle awards are the structural news. NASA confirmed Tuesday it will award contracts for two LTVs as the modern Lunar Roving Vehicle. The first LTV is scheduled to ride a Blue Origin Mark One — the same lander family flying Moon Base 1 — in fall 2027. It will also be the spacecraft that delivers NASA's VIPER rover.

This matters because the LTV is the first piece of Moon Base hardware designed for crew use, not just precursor robotics. The Apollo LRVs were single-mission disposables. The new LTVs are meant to operate semi-autonomously between crew visits, expanding the geographic reach of every Artemis mission that comes after.

NASA is also opening a parallel solicitation for CLPS 2.0, with proposals due June 30 and provider selections "early in the fall, or even late in the summer of 2026," Isaacman said. The agency wants a vendor pool for mid-sized cargo delivery — one to several metric tons — and Garcia Guillen said it is "going to be a very quick turnaround between making those vendors eligible to when we start contracting those missions."

## What the three phases actually mean

Garcia Guillen's three-phase framing is the cleanest summary the program has produced to date.

Phase one (now through 2029) is high-reliability lunar access — getting cargo to the surface dependably, testing survival on the lunar night, and characterizing sites at meter-scale resolution. "It dawns on us every day how little we know of the lunar surface," he said.

Phase two is permanent infrastructure: power, communications, navigation, mid-cargo logistics. Phase three is permanent habitation, and NASA is already taking proposals — not for studies but for actual hardware — to deploy a pathfinder habitat module, test it on the surface, and graduate the design to permanent presence.

The cargo numbers tell the story: four metric tons delivered in phase one, scaling to 60 metric tons in phase two, and 150 metric tons by phase three. For comparison, the entire Apollo program delivered roughly two metric tons across six missions.

## The budget question

Isaacman cited three funding sources when asked how NASA plans to pay for the expanded manifest: the $10 billion plus-up from the Working Family Tax Cut Act ("a large portion of the 10 billion was in exploration"), FY26 appropriations, and the 2027 president's budget request, "which we believe is more than adequate to meet our exploration goals."

"There's strong bipartisan support around the Moon Base," he added. Whether that holds when the FY27 budget moves through Congress is the harder question — and one the briefing did not try to answer.

## Where this leaves Artemis III

Artemis III is no longer the central event of NASA's lunar program. That is the quietest but most consequential implication of Tuesday's briefing. Isaacman confirmed the crew announcement is being prepared and that stacking begins this summer for a target launch in mid-2027. But the Moon Base manifest now stretches in front of, alongside, and beyond Artemis III in a way it did not before Ignition Day.

Artemis III becomes one mission inside a sustained surface program rather than the program itself. The first humans on the south pole will arrive at a place where three robotic missions have already characterized the terrain, where an LTV may already be waiting, and where the next generation of cargo landers is in active flight.

That reframing explains the line Isaacman has now used twice on stage: "Through Artemis, we are going. And with Moon Base, we're going to stay."

## What to watch next

Three concrete things to track over the next four months:

- **CLPS 2.0 proposals close June 30**, with vendor selections in late summer or early fall 2026. The shortlist will tell us which companies NASA expects to anchor mid-cargo delivery for the rest of the decade.
- **Artemis III crew announcement** — Isaacman said it is being prepared. Stacking begins this summer.
- **Blue Origin Mark One Moon Base 1 launch readiness review**. Fall 2026 is the no-earlier-than date, but the lander's first commercial flight will determine whether the Moon Base manifest stays on its current schedule or slips into 2027.

The slogan "this time to stay" has been around since the original Artemis rollout in 2017. Tuesday was the first time NASA published a hardware schedule that could actually deliver on it.
