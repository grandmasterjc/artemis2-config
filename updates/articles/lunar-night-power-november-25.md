The sun set on Firefly Aerospace's Blue Ghost lander on March 16, 2025. The spacecraft had worked through 346 hours of daylight since landing on March 2, running on solar power. After sunset it kept going on its batteries for more than five hours and sent its last data at about 6:15 p.m. Central time, according to Firefly. Then it went silent.

The night lasted two weeks. Blue Ghost was a robot whose mission was planned to end shortly after dusk. A crewed base has to keep people warm and machines running until the sun comes back.

On Nov. 23, NASA asked industry to build its answer: Lunar Reactor-1 (LR-1), a fission reactor producing 20 kilowatts of electricity. "A reliable source of safe and abundant energy will be vital to future exploration of the lunar and Martian surface," said Brian Giblin, the program's manager at NASA. Batteries, decaying plutonium and well-chosen ridges at the south pole each cover part of the night. The reactor is NASA's plan for the rest.

## 354 hours of darkness

The moon turns once on its axis for each trip around Earth, so one cycle of day and night takes about 29.5 Earth days. Away from the poles, each half lasts about two weeks. NASA's solicitation for LR-1 requires the reactor to work through 14.75 days of continuous darkness, according to a summary of the document by the American Nuclear Society. That is 354 hours.

Cold comes with the dark. Near the equator the surface reaches 250 degrees Fahrenheit (120 degrees Celsius) by day and falls to minus 208 F (minus 130 C) at night, according to NASA's Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter team. The moon has no atmosphere to carry heat from the sunlit side.

"The lunar night is challenging from a technical perspective," Trudy Kortes, then NASA's program director for technology demonstration missions, said in 2024. A base needs electricity for all 354 hours, and it needs heat.

## The weight of batteries

The obvious approach is to store solar power for the night. Mass is the obstacle.

Engineers at NASA's Glenn Research Center put packaged lithium-ion battery systems at about 160 watt-hours per kilogram. Supplying LR-1's 20 kilowatts for 354 hours takes 7,080 kilowatt-hours. By our arithmetic from the Glenn figure, batteries holding that much would weigh about 44 metric tons (roughly 97,500 pounds). NASA's Moon Base plan, published in March, budgets up to 60 tons of cargo for the whole of its second phase, spread across as many as 24 landings between 2029 and 2032.

Glenn is developing a lighter store called a regenerative fuel cell. By day, solar power splits water into hydrogen and oxygen. At night a fuel cell recombines the gases and gives back electricity and the water. Such systems range from under 100 to more than 600 watt-hours per kilogram, depending on where they sit and how much energy they have to hold, according to a 2023 Glenn presentation.

## Plutonium heat

Apollo solved a small version of the problem with plutonium-238. The instrument stations left behind by Apollo 12 and Apollo 14 through 17 each drew power from a SNAP-27 generator, which turned the heat of radioactive decay into 70 watts of electricity at the start of its life, according to NASA. An old incandescent light bulb uses about as much.

The generators had a two-year design life. All five ran for five to eight years, day and night, until NASA shut the stations down in 1977.

A smaller relative makes no electricity at all. A radioisotope heater unit is about the size of a C-cell battery, holds a plutonium pellet as big as a pencil eraser and gives off about one watt of heat, NASA says. Placed beside electronics, it keeps them from freezing.

Both devices are in the Moon Base plan. Its first phase, which runs to 2029, includes heater-unit demonstrations "to ensure systems can survive the lunar night." The Moon Base user's guide NASA issued in April adds a goal of surviving the night with radioisotope generators. That is enough to keep a parked rover alive and far too little to run a habitat.

## Shorter nights at the pole

Location does part of the work. At the south pole, where Artemis crews are headed, the sun circles the horizon and high ground stays lit most of the time.

A team led by Philipp Gläser simulated 19 years of lighting on the ridge between Shackleton and de Gerlache craters for a 2014 study. It found spots that are sunlit 92.3 percent of the time at 2 meters (6.6 feet) above the ground and 95.66 percent at 10 meters (33 feet). The longest stretches of darkness there typically last three to five days.

![Hours of darkness on the moon compared. Graphic: Artemis Briefing.](https://grandmasterjc.github.io/artemis2-config/updates/images/lunar-night-power-november-25-darkness.jpg)

So NASA's solar hardware for the pole stands upright. In 2022 the agency awarded $19.4 million to three companies for prototype arrays that deploy up to 32 feet (10 meters) high, because the sun never climbs far above the polar horizon. The user's guide sets a first-phase target to match: 5 kilowatts of generation and storage, and survival through more than 120 hours, or five days, of darkness.

The well-lit ridges are small, and the ice that draws NASA to the pole lies in craters the sun never reaches.

## A reactor on the surface

A fission reactor makes heat by splitting uranium atoms, in sunlight or without it. In LR-1 that heat will drive a closed Brayton cycle, a sealed loop of gas that spins a turbine, and the fuel will be high-assay low-enriched uranium, according to the American Nuclear Society's summary of the solicitation. Heat the turbine cannot use has to be radiated to space from large panels.

![A model of a liquid-metal-cooled reactor, shown at NASA Headquarters in March 2026. Credit: NASA/Bill Ingalls.](https://grandmasterjc.github.io/artemis2-config/updates/images/lunar-night-power-november-25-reactor-model.jpg)

NASA has run a small space reactor on the ground before. From November 2017 through March 2018, its Kilopower experiment ran in Nevada with a uranium core about the size of a paper towel roll, ending in a 28-hour test at full power, according to the agency.

In 2022, three industry teams received $5 million each to design a lunar reactor of 40 kilowatts that weighed under 6 metric tons (13,200 pounds) and ran for a decade untended. At that mass, a reactor twice as powerful as LR-1 would weigh about one-seventh as much as the batteries above.

LR-1 asks for half the power of those designs, which NASA compares to the needs of about 16 average U.S. households. Per the society's summary, the reactor must hold 20 kilowatts after any single failure and run for at least five years with no maintenance. It will not operate at power until it is on the moon. A crew habitat 1 kilometer (0.6 miles) away is allowed no more than 5 rem a year above the moon's natural background, the same figure the U.S. Nuclear Regulatory Commission sets as the yearly limit for radiation workers.

## Proposals, then hardware

NASA set a Dec. 21 deadline for proposals. The companies it selects will have 18 months to test components and to run a full-scale, electrically heated stand-in for the reactor in a vacuum chamber, the American Nuclear Society reported. The agency wants a flight unit ready to launch by December 2030.

In the Moon Base plan, the first nuclear power stations arrive in the second phase, from 2029 to 2032. An operational fission station that carries the base through the night belongs to the third, after 2032.

The smaller demonstrations come sooner. Blue Ghost lasted five hours past sunset. The robotic landers of the plan's first phase will carry heater units and solar stations, and they are meant to still be transmitting when the sun comes back.
