On April 28, the SLS core stage for Artemis III was rolled into the Vehicle Assembly Building at Kennedy Space Center. Hardware in the building is a real milestone — the kind of moment that makes a mission feel close.

But it's been more than two months since NASA rewrote Artemis III on February 27, and several of the questions you'd expect answered by now still aren't.

What orbit will the mission fly in? How long will it last? Who's the crew? Which lander does Orion meet up with first? How will the Axiom spacesuit actually be tested?

NASA hasn't said.

Here's where things actually stand — and why the schedule is starting to drift in ways that matter for Artemis IV.

![Orion meeting an HLS in orbit — the Artemis III concept](https://grandmasterjc.github.io/artemis2-config/updates/images/orion_hls_docking.jpg)

## What's confirmed

The hardware side is on track. The core stage is at KSC. Solid rocket booster segments started arriving in April and the rest are expected by summer. Orion's crew capsule and service module mate this summer. By any reasonable measure, the vehicle for Artemis III will be physically ready for a 2027 launch attempt.

What was confirmed in February:

- Artemis III is no longer a lunar landing. It's a low Earth orbit rendezvous test — analogous to Apollo 9 — where Orion meets the lunar landers from SpaceX and Blue Origin.
- The first crewed lunar landing has moved to Artemis IV in early 2028.
- A second landing is planned for Artemis V in late 2028, using Blue Origin's Blue Moon Mk2.

That's the official posture. Hardware is real, the architecture has been redrawn, and NASA still talks about a 10-month cadence between missions.

## What's still open

Here's the part that's harder to dismiss: the mission profile itself hasn't been published. As of May 2026, all of the following remain undecided or unannounced:

- The mission orbit
- The mission duration
- The crew
- How (and where) the Axiom spacesuit will be tested
- Whether Artemis III flies with or without an upper stage
- Which lander Orion rendezvouses with first, and in what sequence

These are not minor details. They are the inputs that drive Orion's power and thermal margins, the launch windows, the training flow for the crew, and the design of the rendezvous itself.

## Two different timelines

NASA originally targeted Artemis III for mid-2027. In April, the picture became more nuanced.

Kent Chojnacki, deputy program manager for NASA's Human Landing System program, told SpaceNews shortly before the Artemis II launch:

"Our direction is no earlier than March, no later than June" of 2027.

Three weeks later, NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman framed it differently. At a House appropriations hearing on April 27:

"I've received responses from both HLS vendors, both SpaceX and Blue Origin, to meet our needs for a late 2027 rendezvous and docking."

And in an ABC News interview on April 30:

"Some time mid to late 2027 when Artemis 3 would launch."

These aren't contradictory, but they describe different ends of the same window. The HLS office is working a March–June 2027 schedule. The administrator is publicly framing mid-to-late 2027. When two parts of a program quote different ranges this far out, the later one usually prevails.

## Why a few months matter for Artemis IV

NASA wants two crewed lunar landings in 2028 — Artemis IV early in the year and Artemis V late. The 10-month cadence is what makes that possible.

The arithmetic is straightforward.

If Artemis III launches in March 2027, Artemis IV lands around January 2028 and both missions fit inside 2028.

If Artemis III launches in June 2027, Artemis IV lands around April 2028 — still fits, with margin for Artemis V late in the year.

But if Artemis III slips to October 2027, Artemis IV moves to August 2028, and only one lunar landing fits in the year.

If it slips further, to December 2027, Artemis IV doesn't land until October 2028 — and Artemis V is gone.

A modest slip on Artemis III doesn't move Artemis IV by much in absolute terms. But because the 10-month cadence runs from launch to launch, a slip into Q4 2027 effectively removes one of the two planned 2028 landings.

That's why the framing of the Artemis III date matters.

## The orbital rendezvous concept

The new Artemis III also asks something that hasn't been done before: orchestrating Orion in low Earth orbit with two separately developed lunar landers, each requiring its own propellant depot architecture.

Chojnacki described the goal this way:

"What our ultimate goal would be is to be able to do the orbital rendezvous, prox ops, maybe docking with both providers… We have to find a common orbit. We have to find a common launch opportunity, and orchestrating a launch of an SLS, two HLSs will be some kind of feat. We're working on what the art of the possible is there."

In engineering language, "art of the possible" means the geometry hasn't fully closed yet. There's no public answer to whether one rendezvous or two will be attempted, in what sequence, or in what orbit.

## The spacesuit, separately

When NASA rewrote Artemis III, it added Axiom Space's AxEMU spacesuit testing as a mission objective. In April, Axiom said publicly that they don't yet know what that test looks like — and that it might end up happening on the International Space Station instead.

"We've provided the agency with a number of options. It would certainly be a valuable exercise, but we just don't have the specifics at this time," said Russell Ralston, Axiom's senior vice president of EVA.

"The agency has made it clear we're going to fly a suit next year. Is that to the International Space Station? Is that with the HLS providers? To be determined," said Jonathan Cirtain, Axiom's CEO.

The suit is on track to fly in 2027. Where it flies is open.

## Three signals worth watching in the next 90 days

A lot of this resolves itself between now and August. Three things to track.

The crew announcement. Isaacman said on April 30 that NASA is "not far away" from naming the Artemis III crew. The Artemis II crew was announced in April 2023 for a then-planned late-2024 launch — about 18 months out. A crew announcement in May or June is consistent with a late-2027 launch. A slip past July starts to suggest the schedule is real, not just rhetorical.

The ICPS decision. With the Exploration Upper Stage cancelled, NASA is reportedly weighing whether Artemis III flies without an upper stage at all — preserving the last ICPS for Artemis IV. Either decision tells you something about how minimal NASA is willing to make Artemis III to keep the cadence.

The orbit and the landing partner sequence. The first time NASA publishes a target orbit and a rendezvous order, the mission shifts from concept to plan.

## Where this leaves us

The hardware for Artemis III is on track. The mission profile isn't finalized. The program office is targeting March–June 2027; the administrator is signaling mid to late 2027. That gap will close in the next few months, most likely toward the later end.

It's still more than a year away, so there's time. But the answers to the open questions land between now and summer, not in the final weeks before launch. The moment any of them does, you'll see it here first.
