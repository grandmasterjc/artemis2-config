Between Artemis II's splashdown and the planned crewed landing of Artemis III lies one of the most ambitious engineering feats ever attempted in spaceflight — and it has nothing to do with flying to the Moon. It has to do with filling up the spacecraft that will take them there.

## The Architecture Is Different This Time

Apollo was simple by comparison. A single Saturn V launched everything — crew, command module, lunar module, and all the fuel needed to get to the Moon and back — on one rocket. The entire mission fit inside a single launch.

Artemis III abandons that model entirely. Here's what the mission actually requires:

1. A **Space Launch System (SLS)** rocket launches four astronauts in NASA's Orion spacecraft toward the Moon
2. Separately, **SpaceX launches a Starship Human Landing System (HLS)** into low Earth orbit
3. Before Starship HLS can go anywhere, SpaceX must launch **a fleet of tanker Starships** to refill it with propellant in orbit
4. Only then does Starship HLS head to lunar orbit, where Orion meets it
5. Two astronauts transfer to Starship HLS and descend to the surface

The step that has space engineers worried is step 3.

## The Number of Tanker Flights

SpaceX's own documents, obtained by *Politico* in late 2025, estimate that Starship HLS will need **up to 12 refueling flights** in low Earth orbit before it can head to the Moon. Some independent analyses put the number closer to 14 or 15, depending on how much propellant Starship can actually deliver per flight.

Each of those tanker flights has to launch successfully, dock with the fuel depot, transfer cryogenic propellant — and do it all before the propellant already in orbit boils off into space.

This has never been done before. Not by anyone.

## Why Cryogenic Transfer Is So Hard

Starship uses liquid methane and liquid oxygen as propellant. Both must be kept at extreme cold — around -180°C for methane and -183°C for oxygen — or they boil off and escape.

On Earth, loading cryogenic propellant into a rocket is already difficult. In microgravity, with no "up" to settle the liquid against, it becomes a challenge no one has solved at scale.

SpaceX demonstrated a small in-tank propellant transfer during Starship's third test flight in 2024, moving about 10 tons between internal tanks on the same vehicle. The real test — transferring propellant between **two separate vehicles** in orbit — is not planned until mid-2026 at the earliest.

Even if that first demo succeeds, SpaceX must then prove they can do it reliably, at scale, and within a narrow window before boil-off takes too much of their propellant budget.

## NASA's Safety Panel Is Worried

In September 2025, NASA's Aerospace Safety Advisory Panel (ASAP) delivered a blunt assessment after visiting SpaceX's Starbase facility:

> "The HLS schedule is significantly challenged and, in our estimation, could be years late for a 2027 Artemis III Moon landing."

The panel identified three dependencies that all have to work:

- **Starship Version 3** — the next generation of the vehicle, which hasn't flown yet
- **The tanker and depot configurations** — neither of which exists in flight-ready form
- **Raptor Version 3 engines** — still undergoing performance and reliability improvements

Meanwhile, ASAP noted that SpaceX is splitting attention between its Starlink satellite business, which generates revenue, and the HLS program, which doesn't.

## Former NASA Leadership Speaks Up

Mike Griffin, NASA administrator from 2005 to 2009, has become increasingly vocal in his criticism of the Artemis architecture. His argument is mathematical: if each tanker flight has even a small chance of failure or delay, stacking 12–14 of them in a row dramatically reduces the probability of the whole mission succeeding on schedule.

"The probability of mission success is directly correlated with the number of launches in each refueling campaign," Griffin has argued in recent interviews. It's a point SpaceX hasn't publicly disputed — they've instead focused on increasing Starship's cadence to the point where tanker flights become routine.

## The Timeline Nobody Wants to Say Out Loud

NASA still officially targets mid-2027 for Artemis III. Internal SpaceX documents obtained by reporters suggest the company's own best-case estimate is September 2028. Several independent analyses have floated 2029 or later as more realistic.

The *New York Times* reported that some NASA officials privately believe the mission may not fly until 2032 — though SpaceX CEO Elon Musk dismissed the report in his typical style.

## What Has to Go Right in the Next 12 Months

For Artemis III to remain on schedule, several things need to happen in 2026:

- Starship Version 3's first flight (imminent)
- At least one successful ship-to-ship propellant transfer in orbit
- Demonstrated stability of cryogenic propellant in a depot for extended periods
- An uncrewed lunar landing with Starship HLS

If all four happen smoothly, a 2027 crewed landing remains plausible. If any one slips significantly, Artemis III likely joins a long list of delayed spaceflight programs.

## The Bigger Picture

None of this means Artemis III will fail. SpaceX has repeatedly defied skeptics — from reusable rockets to Falcon Heavy to Dragon. The company's appetite for rapid iteration is exactly why NASA chose them in the first place.

But the challenge ahead is genuinely unprecedented. Orbital refueling at the scale Artemis III requires will either become a solved problem in the next 18 months — and unlock a new era of deep space exploration — or it will become the bottleneck that keeps humans off the Moon for another decade.

The next Starship launch will tell us a lot. Follow updates as they happen.
