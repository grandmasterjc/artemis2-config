On August 4, Northrop Grumman announced three new missions to the lunar surface. Lunar Infrastructure Demo, or LID, will fly power, thermal, communications and hosted-payload hardware to the moon's south pole to prove out systems that can survive the two-week lunar night. The company says the technology is already built. "Our ready-to-fly, reliable HALO technologies allow NASA to move faster, putting in place robust infrastructure that can endure the lunar night and establish a strong blueprint for a future Moon Base," said David Schiller, Northrop Grumman's vice president of civil space and sciences, in the company's announcement.

The technology comes from HALO, the Habitation and Logistics Outpost that Northrop Grumman spent seven years building for NASA's Gateway lunar space station. That is the part of the pitch worth slowing down on. HALO itself never flew. It never got close. NASA canceled the Gateway program in March, the module's primary structure had corrosion running through it, and the agency's own independent reviewers had already concluded the project had no realistic chance of meeting its schedule. Whether "ready-to-fly, reliable" accurately describes what is actually being reused, or gives a collapsed program a cleaner name, is worth checking against the record NASA and Northrop Grumman have already put on paper.

## What Northrop Grumman actually announced

Northrop Grumman's August 4 release describes LID-1, LID-2 and LID-3 as missions built on power, data and mechanical interfaces developed for HALO, aimed at maturing surface power, thermal control, autonomy, communications and hosted-payload services near the south pole, where temperatures swing from about minus 334 degrees Fahrenheit (minus 203 degrees Celsius) in permanent shadow to roughly 130 F (54 C) in direct sun. NASA said the missions fall under Moon Base Phase One, the agency's surface-infrastructure push that runs from 2026 through 2029. Neither NASA nor Northrop Grumman gave a launch date for any of the three missions, and neither said what it costs to convert HALO-derived hardware into a lander payload, according to Spaceflight Now's report on the announcement.

## The module the hardware came from

HALO was Gateway's habitation and logistics module, the pressurized cabin astronauts would have lived in during Artemis missions to lunar orbit. Northrop Grumman won the contract in July 2019 for $187 million. By 2021 it had grown into a $1.3 billion firm-fixed-price award. In September 2024, NASA converted it to a cost-plus contract worth $1.8 billion, a structure that shifts financial risk from the contractor to the agency and is typically used when a program has fallen behind a fixed-price schedule. The module's primary structure, built by subcontractor Thales Alenia Space in Turin, Italy, arrived in the United States in April 2025. Northrop Grumman engineers inspecting it found corrosion throughout the structure. By the time NASA issued a stop-work order in April 2026, the contract had reached $1.9 billion, and HALO had still not flown.

## NASA's review board and the inspector general

The corrosion was not the only warning sign. According to a NASA Office of Inspector General (OIG) memorandum on the termination of Artemis-era hardware programs, the Gateway program's independent Standing Review Board found a zero percent likelihood that HALO would meet its launch readiness date, which had already slipped to October 2025 before the board's assessment. The board wrote that the program's "lack of schedule realism may be driving suboptimal engineering decisions during development." The inspector general's own projection, built from Northrop Grumman's monthly schedule reporting, put realistic delivery at July 2031, six years past the original target, with Gateway itself not operational before 2032. NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman announced the Gateway program's cancellation on March 24, redirecting its roughly $20 billion in remaining funding toward a three-phase lunar surface base instead, with Phase One alone budgeted at $10 billion, according to CNBC's coverage of the announcement. HALO was not an isolated write-off. A separate NASA OIG accounting found four canceled Artemis-era hardware programs, HALO among them, together reached $5.9 billion in contract value against an original combined price of $2.9 billion, Space.com reported.

## How much of HALO is actually being reused

None of this necessarily makes Schiller's claim false. HALO's primary structure, the piece with the corrosion, is not what LID appears to be flying; the announcement describes reusing power, avionics, thermal and communications interfaces, the kind of subsystem hardware that can be built and qualified independently of the module it was designed to sit inside. Components can genuinely be flight-ready even when the vehicle built around them never was. But the distinction matters precisely because Northrop Grumman did not draw it. The press release does not say whether LID flies the same physical units built for HALO, newly built copies of that design, or something adapted from it, and it gives no cost or schedule for finding out. A program with a documented, government-reviewed history of missing its own schedule is now asking to be taken at its word that a different set of deliverables, cut from the same design work, will not repeat it.

![The HALO pressure vessel is moved into Northrop Grumman's Gilbert, Arizona facility for final outfitting in April 2025, the arrival at which engineers found corrosion through the primary structure (NASA)](https://grandmasterjc.github.io/artemis2-config/updates/images/halo-module-arizona-2025.jpg)

The claim is testable, and nothing published so far settles it. NASA has flown hardware built by troubled programs before and had it work fine once it was decoupled from an unrealistic master schedule. It has also relabeled slipping programs as something newer and leaner without fixing what caused the slip. LID is a small enough, cheap enough set of missions that either outcome is plausible.

## What to watch

The clearest test is the one NASA and Northrop Grumman have not yet given a date for: an actual LID launch. Watch for NASA to name a target window inside the 2026-through-2029 Moon Base Phase One period, and for either party to disclose what converting the hardware costs, a number that would show how much new engineering separates "ready-to-fly" from HALO's frozen-in-place primary structure. Watch also for the next NASA OIG report on Artemis termination costs, which could confirm or complicate the $5.9 billion figure as more canceled-program accounting closes out. And watch whether Canada's Space Agency, which had committed robotic and life-support contributions to Gateway, attaches any of that work to the LID missions, a detail so far absent from both the NASA and Northrop Grumman announcements.

None of that resolves the question the August 4 release raised on its own. HALO spent seven years and $1.9 billion without ever flying, and NASA's own review board said, in writing, that it had no realistic path to its own deadline. Whether the hardware Northrop Grumman is now sending to the moon actually escapes that history, or just carries a new mission name, is something only a launch date and a real cost figure can settle, and neither has arrived yet.
