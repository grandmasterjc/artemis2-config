At noon near the lunar equator, where Apollo astronauts walked, the ground reaches almost 400 kelvins, about 260 degrees Fahrenheit (127 degrees Celsius). That is hotter than boiling water. By night the same patch of ground drops below minus 280 degrees Fahrenheit (minus 173 degrees Celsius). And on the floors of craters near the poles that never see sunlight, Diviner, an instrument on the National Aeronautics and Space Administration's (NASA) Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter, has measured temperatures as low as 25 kelvins, or minus 414 degrees Fahrenheit (minus 248 degrees Celsius), [according to the instrument team](https://www.diviner.ucla.edu/science) at the University of California, Los Angeles. [NASA puts Pluto's average](https://science.nasa.gov/dwarf-planets/pluto/facts/) at minus 387 degrees Fahrenheit (minus 232 degrees Celsius).

Artemis astronauts are headed for the lunar south pole, where the sun hangs low on the horizon. Crater rims there that stay in near-constant sunlight hold at about 220 kelvins, or minus 64 degrees Fahrenheit (minus 53 degrees Celsius), Diviner has found, while the shadowed floors below them are among the coldest places measured in the solar system. Axiom Space, which is building the suit they will wear, says its Axiom Extravehicular Mobility Unit (AxEMU) will support moonwalks of at least eight hours and can spend at least two hours inside those permanently shadowed craters, [according to the company's suit unveiling](https://www.axiomspace.com/release/axiom-space-prada-unveil-spacesuit-design-for-moon-return) in October 2024. How it manages that starts with a fact that surprises most people. The suit's hardest thermal job is getting rid of the astronaut's own heat.

## Heat without air

On Earth, most of the heat we lose goes into the air around us. Air carries it away from skin by convection, and a cold wind makes that happen faster. The moon has no air, so none of that happens.

In a vacuum, heat moves in only two ways. It radiates, as infrared light, from anything warm to anything colder. And it conducts through direct contact, from boot sole to ground or from glove to rock. Everything a spacesuit does to manage temperature comes down to controlling those two paths.

Sunlight is the main source of incoming radiation. With no atmosphere to filter it, it arrives at full strength, roughly 1,360 watts on every square meter facing the sun. That is why lunar suits are white. NASA's educational material on spacesuit cooling notes that suits are made from colors and materials that reflect as much of that energy as possible, and Axiom describes the AxEMU's outer layer as a white material that reflects heat and protects against lunar dust.

## The insulation problem

Under the white outer fabric, a spacesuit carries many thin layers of insulation, a design that blocks radiated heat far better than a thick padded coat would. It keeps the sun's heat out and the astronaut's heat in. That second part becomes a problem.

A working astronaut produces a great deal of heat. Walking in a pressurized suit, kneeling and swinging a rock hammer is hard physical labor, and all of that energy ends up as body heat. With the insulation doing its job, that heat has nowhere to go. [A NASA lesson on spacesuit cooling](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2021/03/544886main_ls5_cool_space_suits_c1_final3.pdf) compares it to walking around in summer wearing a plastic bag. Without active cooling, the astronaut would overheat long before the cold outside became a danger.

## Cooling by evaporation

The answer, since Apollo, has been water. Astronauts wear a close-fitting garment laced with small tubes, and water pumped through those tubes picks up heat from the skin. The Smithsonian's National Air and Space Museum holds an Apollo-era version, [a liquid cooling garment built by ILC Industries](https://airandspace.si.edu/collection-objects/liquid-cooling-garment-s-067-apollo/nasm_A19730120000). Axiom unveiled its own version in June, [designed with Prada](https://www.collectspace.com/news/news-060726a-axiom-space-prada-axemu-lcvg.html), with tubes running along the body's major muscle groups. Unlike the Apollo garment, it has a second, fully redundant cooling circuit.

The warmed water then travels to the backpack, where the suit dumps the heat by letting a little water escape into space. Apollo suits and the spacesuits used today on the International Space Station do this with a sublimator. Water seeps through a porous metal plate, freezes on the side facing vacuum, and the ice turns directly into vapor, carrying heat away as it goes.

NASA's newer design swaps the plate for a device called a Spacesuit Water Membrane Evaporator. Cooling water flows through bundles of hollow fibers whose walls repel liquid but let vapor pass. Some of the water evaporates straight through into space, and the rest comes back chilled. Axiom's suit builds on this NASA design, the Exploration Extravehicular Mobility Unit (xEMU). In [a 2024 NASA test report](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20240004206), engineers ran the xEMU's main cooling loop at heat loads up to 900 watts, several times what a person gives off sitting still. A smaller backup loop handled up to 300 watts. Through a simulated eight-hour moonwalk that alternated hot and cold conditions, both loops returned water to the astronaut at about 50 degrees Fahrenheit (10 degrees Celsius).

## Boots, gloves and shadow

In the permanently shadowed craters, the problem changes. There is no sunlight to reflect, and the insulation now works to hold heat in. The weak points are the places where the suit touches the ground: the boots the astronaut stands on and the gloves that pick up rocks. At minus 414 degrees Fahrenheit, the crater floor is colder than liquid nitrogen, and conduction through a boot sole never stops.

Apollo engineers had to insulate against the same kind of contact, only on sunbaked ground. The overshoes Apollo 17 commander Eugene Cernan wore on the moon, now at the Smithsonian, had [extra layers of thermal protection and felt in the soles](https://airandspace.si.edu/collection-objects/boot-left-lunar-overshoe-cernan-apollo-17-flown/nasm_A19740133005) to guard against extreme temperatures and sharp rocks. The Artemis suits will face far colder ground. Axiom gives its shadowed-crater capability as a time, at least two hours, and has not published a figure for longer stays.

## Tested in a vacuum chamber

The only way to check those numbers before a moonwalk is to recreate the conditions on the ground. In November 2025, Axiom and KBR finished the AxEMU's [first uncrewed thermal vacuum test](https://www.axiomspace.com/release/axemu-first-uncrewed-thermal-vacuum-test) at KBR's Aerospace Environment Protection Laboratory in San Antonio. The test put the suit's pressure garment in vacuum at extreme temperatures to evaluate the performance of its new materials, including the two-hour requirement for shadowed regions. Russell Ralston, who leads Axiom's spacewalk program, said it was important to check how those new materials behaved thermally.

Tests like these can prove the insulation and the cooling loop. The final test will come on the lunar surface, where no chamber can fully reproduce the combination of harsh sunlight, deep shadow and abrasive dust that the suit will face within a single moonwalk.

The first people to wear the AxEMU on the moon are expected to fly on Artemis 4, the landing NASA is targeting for 2028. If the AxEMU performs like the NASA design it builds on, the astronauts will work in full sunlight on the crater rims and then step down onto ground colder than Pluto, while the water in their cooling garment stays at about 50 degrees Fahrenheit.
