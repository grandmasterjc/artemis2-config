On SpaceX's first earnings call since going public, on August 4, Elon Musk said the company intends to fly Starship again before the month is out, carry its first operational Starlink V3 satellites to orbit, and try to catch the returning upper stage with the launch tower. All three would be firsts. All three came with a caveat: "assuming we receive regulatory approval to do so," and a date he called tentative ([SpaceNews, Aug. 4, 2026](https://spacenews.com/spacex-to-begin-starship-orbital-flights/)).

That is the case for American acceleration, made by the person with the most riding on it. The case against arrived twelve days earlier. A Government Accountability Office review published July 23 found SpaceX's work on the Human Landing System running more than a year behind its own internal schedule, naming the Raptor engine and the cryogenic hardware that keeps propellant cold in orbit as top risks ([GAO-26-108556](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-26-108556)). And on Face the Nation in early July, NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman conceded the larger point without being pressed. "The Chinese are moving at incredible speeds," he said, "and they are certainly capable of doing what the Soviets were not during the first space race" ([CBS News, July 5, 2026](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/nasa-jared-isaacman-space-race-china/)).

Set all of that against the claim that has circulated since February, when NASA restructured Artemis 3 out of the landing business, and the conclusion looks obvious: the United States is losing the moon race. On the one measure that exists in writing, each side's own published target date, it isn't true. NASA's stated date for a crewed landing still sits about two years ahead of China's. What has changed is how much confidence anyone, including NASA's own administrator, places in that number.

## What actually changed in February

On February 27, Isaacman told reporters that NASA would no longer attempt a crewed lunar landing on Artemis 3. Under the revised plan it becomes a low Earth orbit rehearsal: Orion launches with four crew and rendezvouses with one or both commercial landers, SpaceX's Starship HLS and Blue Origin's Blue Moon Mark 2, to prove out docking and crew transfer before anyone rides one down. NASA's mission page now describes the flight as designed to "demonstrate critical systems for lunar landing... beginning with Artemis IV" ([NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/mission/artemis-iii/)). The crew, named June 9, pairs Andre Douglas, Frank Rubio and Randy Bresnik with Luca Parmitano of the European Space Agency (ESA). The landing itself now falls to Artemis 4, no earlier than 2028.

Hardware keeps moving regardless of what the mission is called. Solid rocket booster segments for Artemis 3 have been going vertical in the Vehicle Assembly Building since July 11, and all four RS-25 engines were inside the building by June 16, with L3Harris confirming the handoff on July 22 ([L3Harris](https://www.l3harris.com/newsroom/press-release/2026/07/l3harris-delivers-sls-core-stage-engines-artemis-iii-mission)). Technicians are bolting them into the stage vertically, which no Space Launch System (SLS) mission has done before. These are also the last four RS-25s built from leftover space shuttle inventory, closing out a supply chain that has powered every SLS launch to date.

## What China has actually flown, and what hasn't happened yet

China's path to the surface runs through three new vehicles: the Long March 10 rocket, the Mengzhou crew capsule and the Lanyue lander. None has flown a full orbital mission. What China has completed over the past year is a run of ground and low-altitude qualification tests, ending with a low-altitude flight test of the Long March 10 system at Wenchang on February 11 ([Global Times](https://www.globaltimes.cn/page/202602/1355194.shtml)). Each is a genuine step. None is crewed, and none is orbital.

The next concrete date on China's lunar calendar belongs to a robotic mission. Chang'e-7, built to search the lunar south pole for water ice, reached the Wenchang launch site in April; officials have confirmed only a second-half-2026 window ([SpaceNews](https://spacenews.com/chinas-change-7-arrives-at-spaceport-for-lunar-south-pole-exploration-mission/)). Chinese officials have not hidden the risk still ahead of the crewed program either. Zhang Jingbo, of China's human spaceflight effort, said the agency remains "steadfast in our goal of achieving a Chinese lunar landing by 2030," while acknowledging that "many new technologies still need to be verified... progress is tight, presenting various risks and challenges" ([SpaceNews, Oct. 30, 2025](https://spacenews.com/china-targets-2026-for-first-long-march-10-launch-new-lunar-crew-spacecraft-flight/)).

## The dates, compared honestly

Line up the two programs' own numbers and NASA still wins. Artemis 4 is targeting a crewed landing no earlier than 2028. China has committed only to "before 2030." Take both agencies at their word and the United States gets there roughly two years sooner.

The honest complication is how much weight either number can bear. NASA's inspector general found in March that SpaceX's HLS Starship work was already delayed by at least two years, with more delays expected, and that the ship-to-ship propellant transfer demonstration the whole architecture depends on, originally due in March 2025, had already slipped to March 2026 ([NASA Office of Inspector General, IG-26-004, March 10, 2026](https://oig.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/final-report-ig-26-004-nasas-management-of-the-human-landing-system-contracts.pdf)). That revised date has since passed too, and the demonstration had still not flown as of Starship's most recent test in late July. China's public tests, by contrast, have landed roughly on the schedule Beijing set a year ago. A two-year lead built on a schedule that keeps slipping is different from one built on a schedule that has mostly held.

## Why the agency's own chief keeps sounding the alarm anyway

Isaacman has been making a version of this argument since before he had the job. At his second Senate confirmation hearing, in December, he told the Commerce, Science and Transportation Committee that "we are in a great competition with a rival that has the will and the means to challenge American exceptionalism across multiple domains, including in the high ground of space," warning that "if we make a mistake, we may never catch up" ([SpaceNews, Dec. 3, 2025](https://spacenews.com/isaacman-senators-emphasize-urgency-in-returning-humans-to-the-moon/)). Seven months into the job, with the flagship mission already restructured and a GAO report about to land on his desk, his tone in July hadn't softened.

That consistency matters more than either quote alone. A nominee arguing for confirmation has an obvious reason to sound alarmed about a rival. An administrator seven months in, after personally pulling the landing off the next mission, has less reason to keep saying it unless he believes it. His target in both settings is NASA's own calendar, not China's.

## Five milestones to watch

A short list of tests will settle more of this than any speech. Flight 14, the one Musk put at the end of this month, would attempt Starship's first orbital insertion and the first tower catch of a returning ship, both milestones the HLS timeline has been waiting on. The propellant transfer demonstration has no confirmed date and is the single biggest swing factor in whether Artemis 4 holds for 2028. Watch for confirmation that all four RS-25 engines are fully bolted into the Artemis 3 core stage. On China's side, watch Wenchang for Chang'e-7's actual launch, and watch for word of a Long March 10 orbital-class test, the step that would move Mengzhou and Lanyue out of ground testing.

None of that makes the GAO's July findings comfortable reading inside NASA, and none of it makes Isaacman's answer on television sound like posturing. The schedule on paper still belongs to the United States, by about two years. Whether that gap survives the next eighteen months depends on hardware that has already slipped once. China's timeline looks slower on paper, and so far it's the one nobody has had to revise.
